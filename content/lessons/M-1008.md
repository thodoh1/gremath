## 3. Precise statement

Off-by-one: the number of integers from $m$ to $n$ inclusive is $n-m+1$. The number of terms in $a,a+d,\ldots,a+kd$ is $k+1$. Open vs closed intervals: a Riemann sum with $n$ panels has $n+1$ nodes. Geometric: $n$ distinct interior cuts of a stick produce $n+1$ pieces. Power series starting at $n=0$ vs $n=1$ after differentiation. Fencepost: $n$ fenceposts, $n-1$ gaps.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** How many integers $n$ satisfy $3\le n\le 10$?

**Solution.** $8$, not $7$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** Sum of $1+3+5+\cdots+(2n-1)$ has how many terms?

**Solution.** $n$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Using $n-m$ for the count of integers in $[m,n]$, or dropping the $n=0$ term of a geometric series after writing $1/(1-r)$.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Number of subintervals of width $h=(b-a)/n$ in a composite rule on $[a,b]$.

**Solution.** $n$ panels, $n+1$ nodes.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** How many integers satisfy $-2\le k\le 2$?

**Solution.** $5$.
