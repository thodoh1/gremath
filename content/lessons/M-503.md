# Lesson M-503 — Euclidean algorithm

Part L. L. Number Theory · **Divisibility & primes** · High

Prerequisites: `A.1-A.3`.


This lesson isolates **Euclidean algorithm** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a High skill: it is standard GRE material. You should be able to do it without warming up. Number theory is additional-topics / algebra: congruences, Euler, CRT.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Euclidean algorithm**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 3. Precise statement

Euclidean algorithm: $\gcd(a,b)=\gcd(b,a\bmod b)$, iterating until remainder $0$; the last nonzero remainder is the gcd. It computes $\gcd$ without factoring. The number of steps is $O(\log\max(|a|,|b|))$.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Compute $\gcd(252,198)$ by the Euclidean algorithm.

**Solution.** $252=1\cdot 198+54$, $198=3\cdot 54+36$, $54=1\cdot 36+18$, $36=2\cdot 18+0$. Last nonzero remainder $18$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** $\gcd(1071,462)$ equals

**Solution.** $21$: $1071=2\cdot 462+147$, $462=3\cdot 147+21$, $147=7\cdot 21$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Stopping at a remainder that still has a common factor rather than continuing to $0$.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** $\gcd(1001,91)$.

**Solution.** $91$, because $1001=11\cdot 91$.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Why $\gcd(a,b)=\gcd(b,a-qb)$?

**Solution.** Any common divisor of $a,b$ divides $a-qb$, and conversely.
