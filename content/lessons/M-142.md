# Lesson M-142 — Principal-value conventions

Part C. C. Trigonometry & Conics · **Trigonometric equations & inequalities** · Critical

Prerequisites: `C.1-C.2`.


This lesson isolates **Principal-value conventions** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a Critical skill: it is used constantly, and a hole here leaks points all over the paper. Trigonometry appears in calculus, geometry, and as standalone identity/equation items. Exact values and identities are speed.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Principal-value conventions**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Trigonometric equations & inequalities*

Because of periodicity, trig equations have infinitely many solutions unless the domain is restricted. General solutions add $2\pi k$ or $\pi k$ according to the function. Inverse trig functions return principal values; they are not full inverse relations. Always state the range convention.

### This skill, specifically

Because of periodicity, trig equations have infinitely many solutions unless the domain is restricted.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

Principal values are conventions: GRE and calculus textbooks use the ranges in M-140. Some older sources use $\mathrm{Arcsin}$ vs $\arcsin$ inconsistently. Always state the range.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Why is $\arccos(-1/2)=2\pi/3$ not $-2\pi/3$?

**Solution.** Because $\arccos$ lands in $[0,\pi]$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** The calculator’s $\mathrm{atan2}(y,x)$ vs $\arctan(y/x)$.

**Solution.** $\mathrm{atan2}$ knows the quadrant; $\arctan$ of a ratio does not.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Mixing two conventions in one solution.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Principal value of $\mathrm{Arg}(-1)$.

**Solution.** $\pi$, not $-\pi$, in the usual $(-\pi,\pi]$ or $[0,2\pi)$ — GRE analysis usually $(-\pi,\pi]$, so $\pi$.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Is $\arcsin$ odd?

**Solution.** Yes: $\arcsin(-x)=-\arcsin x$.
