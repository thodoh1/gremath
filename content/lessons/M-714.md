# Lesson M-714 — Nested quantifiers

Part Q. Q. Logic, Sets & Proof · **Predicate logic** · High

Prerequisites: `Q.1`.


This lesson isolates **Nested quantifiers** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a High skill: it is standard GRE material. You should be able to do it without warming up. Logic and proof are how other items are worded. Counting and analysis both depend on quantifiers.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Nested quantifiers**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Predicate logic*

Predicates, $\forall,\exists$, nested quantifiers, negation rules ($\neg\forall=\exists\neg$). Necessary vs sufficient is $\to$ direction. This is how $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ is written.

### This skill, specifically

Predicates, $\forall,\exists$, nested quantifiers, negation rules ($\neg\forall=\exists\neg$).

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

Order of nested quantifiers matters: $\forall x\exists y\, P(x,y)$ allows $y$ to depend on $x$; $\exists y\forall x\, P(x,y)$ demands a single $y$ working for all $x$. Convergence is $\forall\varepsilon>0\,\exists N\,\forall n\ge N$. Swapping $\forall\varepsilon$ with $\exists N$ yields a much stronger ``$N$ independent of $\varepsilon$'' statement.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Compare $\forall x\in\mathbb{R}\,\exists y,\, y>x$ with $\exists y\forall x,\, y>x$.

**Solution.** The first is true (take $y=x+1$). The second is false (no real larger than every real).

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** In $a_n\to 0$, may $N$ depend on $\varepsilon$?

**Solution.** Yes: that is $\forall\varepsilon\exists N$. It may not depend on $n$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Illegally pulling $\exists$ in front of $\forall$ (``there is $N$ for every $\varepsilon$'' misread as independent of $\varepsilon$).
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** $\forall\varepsilon>0\,\exists N,\, 1/N<\varepsilon$ is Archimedes. $\exists N\forall\varepsilon>0,\, 1/N<\varepsilon$ would mean

**Solution.** $1/N=0$, impossible.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Continuity vs uniform continuity in quantifiers.

**Solution.** Cont: $\forall a\forall\varepsilon\exists\delta\forall x$. Uniform: $\forall\varepsilon\exists\delta\forall a\forall x$ ($\delta$ independent of $a$).

## 9. Lock it in

You own this skill when you can do Drill 1 and Drill 2 tomorrow, mixed among unrelated problems, in under five minutes each, with the traps named in Section 7 avoided on purpose.
