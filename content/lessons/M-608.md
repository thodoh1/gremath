# Lesson M-608 — Submodules

Part N. N. Rings, Modules & Fields · **Modules** · Medium

Prerequisites: `N.1`.


This lesson isolates **Submodules** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a Medium skill: it appears less often, but when it appears it is often a discriminator. Rings and fields appear as recognition and finite examples, especially $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ and $F[x]$.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Submodules**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Modules*

Modules are vector spaces over rings. Submodules, quotients, homomorphisms, generators, free modules. When the ring is a field, you recover linear algebra.

### This skill, specifically

Modules are vector spaces over rings.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

A submodule $N\subseteq M$ is an additive subgroup with $R N\subseteq N$. Examples: subspaces; ideals as submodules of $R$; $n\mathbb{Z}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}$; $p$-torsion in an abelian group. Intersections and sums of submodules are submodules. Cyclic submodule $Rm=\{rm:r\in R\}$.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Submodules of $\mathbb{Z}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module, and of $F^2$ as an $F[x]$-module via a matrix?

**Solution.** Submodules of $\mathbb{Z}$ are $n\mathbb{Z}$. For $F^2$ with $x$ acting by a linear operator $T$, submodules are $T$-invariant subspaces.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** $2\mathbb{Z}+3\mathbb{Z}$ as a submodule of $\mathbb{Z}$

**Solution.** $\mathbb{Z}$, because $2,3$ generate $1$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Checking additive closure but not absorption of scalars (a subgroup of an $F$-vector space that is not a subspace).
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Is $\mathbb{Q}$ a submodule of $\mathbb{R}$ as $\mathbb{Z}$-modules?

**Solution.** Yes: an additive subgroup closed under $\mathbb{Z}$.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Cyclic submodule generated by $2$ in $\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$.

**Solution.** $\{0,2,4,6\}\cong\mathbb{Z}/4$.

## 9. Lock it in

You own this skill when you can do Drill 1 and Drill 2 tomorrow, mixed among unrelated problems, in under five minutes each, with the traps named in Section 7 avoided on purpose.
