## 3. Precise statement

Closed form $\leftrightarrow$ series: geometric $\sum_{n=0}^\infty r^n=1/(1-r)$ for $|r|<1$; $\dfrac{1}{1-z}=\sum z^n$; $\sum n r^{n-1}=1/(1-r)^2$; Taylor of $e^z,\sin z,\cos z,\ln(1+z)$. Generating functions turn recurrences into algebra. On GRE, summing $\sum n x^n$ is usually ‘differentiate the geometric series’, not a fresh induction.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Sum $\sum_{n=1}^\infty n/2^n$.

**Solution.** $\sum n r^n=r/(1-r)^2$ at $r=1/2$ gives $2$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** Taylor of $1/(1+x^2)$ at $0$ is

**Solution.** $\sum_{n=0}^\infty (-1)^n x^{2n}$ for $|x|<1$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Using $\sum r^n=1/(1-r)$ at $r=2$, or differentiating without shifting the index and then plugging $r=1$.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** $\sum_{n=0}^\infty \dfrac{x^n}{n!}=$

**Solution.** $e^x$ (all $x$).

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Partial sums of $\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} r^k$ for $r\neq 1$.

**Solution.** $(1-r^n)/(1-r)$.
