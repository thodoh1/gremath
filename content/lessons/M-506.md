# Lesson M-506 — Number of divisors

Part L. L. Number Theory · **Divisibility & primes** · High

Prerequisites: `A.1-A.3`.


This lesson isolates **Number of divisors** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a High skill: it is standard GRE material. You should be able to do it without warming up. Number theory is additional-topics / algebra: congruences, Euler, CRT.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Number of divisors**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Divisibility & primes*

$a\mid b$, division algorithm, gcd, lcm, Euclid, Bézout, unique factorization, $\tau$ and $\sigma$ from prime factors. This is the arithmetic of $\mathbb{Z}$ as a Euclidean domain.

### This skill, specifically

$a\mid b$, division algorithm, gcd, lcm, Euclid, Bézout, unique factorization, $\tau$ and $\sigma$ from prime factors.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

If $n=\prod p_i^{e_i}$, the number of positive divisors is $\tau(n)=d(n)=\prod(e_i+1)$. Each divisor is $\prod p_i^{a_i}$ with $0\le a_i\le e_i$. $d$ is multiplicative: $\gcd(m,n)=1\Rightarrow d(mn)=d(m)d(n)$.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** How many positive divisors has $360=2^3 3^2 5$?

**Solution.** $(3+1)(2+1)(1+1)=4\cdot 3\cdot 2=24$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** $d(p^{k})$ for $p$ prime equals

**Solution.** $k+1$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Adding the exponents instead of adding $1$ to each, or counting $1$ and $n$ twice.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** $d(84)$ for $84=2^2\cdot 3\cdot 7$.

**Solution.** $(2+1)(1+1)(1+1)=12$.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Which $n<20$ has the most divisors?

**Solution.** $12$ and $18$ each have $6$; $16=2^4$ has $5$. So $12$ or $18$.

## 9. Lock it in

You own this skill when you can do Drill 1 and Drill 2 tomorrow, mixed among unrelated problems, in under five minutes each, with the traps named in Section 7 avoided on purpose.
