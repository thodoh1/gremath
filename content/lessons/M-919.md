# Lesson M-919 — Central angles

Part V. V. Geometry · **Circle & polygon geometry** · High

Prerequisites: `V.1-C.5`.


This lesson isolates **Central angles** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a High skill: it is standard GRE material. You should be able to do it without warming up. Geometry is additional topics plus analytic geometry inside calculus/algebra items.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Central angles**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 3. Precise statement

A central angle is an angle with vertex at the center; its measure equals the measure of its intercepted arc. A full circle is $360^\circ$ or $2\pi$ radians. Equal central angles cut equal arcs and equal chords in the same circle. Arc measure is the central angle, not the chord length.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Central angle $60^\circ$ in a circle of radius $6$. Minor arc measure and chord length?

**Solution.** Arc $60^\circ$; chord is a side of an equilateral triangle, length $6$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** A central angle of $\pi/3$ radians intercepts what fraction of the circumference?

**Solution.** $1/6$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Equating chord length with arc length, or using inscribed-angle measure as if it were central.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.
