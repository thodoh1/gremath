# Lesson M-596 — First isomorphism theorem for rings

Part N. N. Rings, Modules & Fields · **Ideals & quotient rings** · Medium

Prerequisites: `N.1`.


This lesson isolates **First isomorphism theorem for rings** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a Medium skill: it appears less often, but when it appears it is often a discriminator. Rings and fields appear as recognition and finite examples, especially $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ and $F[x]$.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **First isomorphism theorem for rings**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Ideals & quotient rings*

Ideals are kernels of ring maps. Principal, prime, maximal. $R/I$ is a field iff $I$ is maximal (in commutative unital rings). Polynomial ideals in $F[x]$ are principal.

### This skill, specifically

Ideals are kernels of ring maps.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

First isomorphism theorem for rings: if $\phi:R\to S$ is a ring hom, then $R/\ker\phi\cong\mathrm{im}\,\phi$ as rings. To prove $R/I\cong S$, produce a surjective hom $R\to S$ with kernel $I$. Examples: $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$; $F[x]/(x-a)\cong F$; $\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x^2+1)\cong\mathbb{Z}[i]$; $\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2+1)\cong\mathbb{C}$.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Prove $\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x^2+1)\cong\mathbb{Z}[i]$.

**Solution.** Evaluation at $i$: $\phi(f)=f(i)$ is a surjective ring hom $\mathbb{Z}[x]\to\mathbb{Z}[i]$. Kernel is polynomials with $f(i)=0$, i.e. $(x^2+1)$ because $x^2+1$ is the min poly of $i$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ and is monic, so it generates the kernel over $\mathbb{Z}$ as well.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** $\mathbb{R}[x]/(x)\cong$

**Solution.** $\mathbb{R}$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Producing an additive-group iso and calling it a ring iso without checking multiplication.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** $F[x]/(x^2)\cong$ polynomials $a+bx$ with $x^2=0$. Is it a domain?

**Solution.** No: $\bar x\neq 0$ but $\bar x^2=0$.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Kernel of $\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}[i]/(1+i)$, reduction. Quotient?

**Solution.** $(1+i)$ has norm $2$, quotient $\cong\mathbb{Z}/2$.

## 9. Lock it in

You own this skill when you can do Drill 1 and Drill 2 tomorrow, mixed among unrelated problems, in under five minutes each, with the traps named in Section 7 avoided on purpose.
