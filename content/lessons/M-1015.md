## 3. Precise statement

Draw the figure or write a $2\times 2$ / $n=2$ example. A geometry stem without a sketch is a trap; a combinatorics formula should be tested on $n=2,3$. A claimed kernel dimension should be checked on a matrix you write down. Tiny examples convert abstract wording into arithmetic.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Claim: the number of subsets of an $n$-set is $n^2$. Test $n=2$.

**Solution.** A $2$-set has $4=2^2$ subsets, accidental; $n=1$ has $2\neq 1$. False. (True count $2^n$.)

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** $|z-1|+|z+1|=4$: sketch first. This is

**Solution.** an ellipse with foci $\pm 1$ and major axis $4$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Trusting a mental picture of a $3$D intersection without drawing the traces in coordinate planes.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.
