# Lesson M-623 — Algebraic closure concept

Part N. N. Rings, Modules & Fields · **Fields & extensions** · Medium

Prerequisites: `N.3-L.2`.


This lesson isolates **Algebraic closure concept** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a Medium skill: it appears less often, but when it appears it is often a discriminator. Rings and fields appear as recognition and finite examples, especially $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ and $F[x]$.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Algebraic closure concept**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Fields & extensions*

A field has every nonzero element invertible. Extensions $K/F$ have degree $[K:F]=\dim_F K$. Algebraic vs transcendental, minimal polynomials, splitting fields, $\mathbb{F}_p$ and $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$.

### This skill, specifically

A field has every nonzero element invertible.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

An algebraic closure $\overline{F}$ of $F$ is an algebraic extension that is algebraically closed: every nonconstant polynomial in $\overline{F}[x]$ has a root in $\overline{F}$ (equivalently splits completely). Existence and uniqueness up to (non-canonical) isomorphism use Zorn. $\mathbb{C}$ is algebraically closed (FTA) and is an algebraic closure of $\mathbb{R}$, but not of $\mathbb{Q}$ (transcendentals). The algebraic numbers $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\subset\mathbb{C}$ form an algebraic closure of $\mathbb{Q}$.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Why is $\mathbb{C}$ not an algebraic closure of $\mathbb{Q}$? What is?

**Solution.** $\pi\in\mathbb{C}$ is transcendental over $\mathbb{Q}$, so $\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{Q}$ is not algebraic. $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:z\text{ algebraic over }\mathbb{Q}\}$ is an algebraic closure of $\mathbb{Q}$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** Algebraic closure of $\mathbb{R}$

**Solution.** $\mathbb{C}$, degree $2$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Claiming every algebraically closed field is $\mathbb{C}$, or that $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}=\mathbb{C}$.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Is a finite field algebraically closed?

**Solution.** No: $x^{q}-x$ has $q$ roots, but $x^q-x+1$ or simply the polynomial $x^q-x$ does not exhaust all; more cleanly $x^{q^n}-x$ for $n>1$ introduces new roots. A finite field of order $q$ does not contain a root of an irreducible of degree $>1$.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Every polynomial over $\mathbb{C}$ of positive degree has

**Solution.** a root in $\mathbb{C}$ (FTA).
