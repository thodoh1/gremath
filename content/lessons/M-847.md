## 3. Precise statement

For a random vector $\mathbf{X}=(X_1,\ldots,X_d)$, the covariance matrix $\Sigma$ has $\Sigma_{ij}=\mathrm{Cov}(X_i,X_j)$. $\Sigma$ is symmetric and positive semidefinite. Diagonal entries are variances. For $A$ a matrix, $\mathrm{Cov}(A\mathbf{X})=A\Sigma A^T$. Multivariate normal is determined by mean and $\Sigma$.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** $\mathrm{Var} X=4$, $\mathrm{Var} Y=9$, $\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=2$. Write $\Sigma$.

**Solution.** $\begin{pmatrix}4&2\\2&9\end{pmatrix}$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** Is $\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&1\end{pmatrix}$ a possible covariance matrix?

**Solution.** No: eigenvalues $3$ and $-1$; not PSD. Also $|\rho|=2>1$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Writing a covariance matrix that is not symmetric, or putting correlation (not covariance) off-diagonal while putting variance on-diagonal.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.
