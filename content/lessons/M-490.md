# Lesson M-490 — Orthonormal bases

Part K. K. Linear Algebra · **Inner-product spaces** · High

This lesson isolates **Orthonormal bases** and trains it until it is automatic.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Orthonormal bases**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Inner-product spaces*

Inner products give geometry: orthogonality, norms, projections, Gram–Schmidt, least squares. Symmetric real matrices are orthogonally diagonalizable (spectral theorem). SVD is the rectangular cousin.

### This skill, specifically

## 3. Precise statement

An orthonormal basis satisfies $\langle u_i,u_j\rangle=\delta_{ij}$. Coordinates are Fourier coefficients: $v=\sum\langle v,u_i\rangle u_i$. Parseval: $\|v\|^2=\sum|\langle v,u_i\rangle|^2$. An orthogonal set of nonzero vectors becomes orthonormal by dividing by norms. Orthonormal sets are linearly independent.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Verify that $\bigl(\frac1{\sqrt{2}},\frac1{\sqrt{2}}\bigr)$, $\bigl(-\frac1{\sqrt{2}},\frac1{\sqrt{2}}\bigr)$ is orthonormal, and expand $(1,0)$.

**Solution.** Each has norm $1$, dot product $0$. Coefficients $\frac1{\sqrt{2}}$ and $-\frac1{\sqrt{2}}$, and $\frac1{\sqrt{2}}u_1-\frac1{\sqrt{2}}u_2=(1,0)$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** If $\{u_1,u_2\}$ is orthonormal, $\langle 3u_1-u_2,u_1\rangle$ equals

**Solution.** $3$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Calling an orthogonal basis orthonormal without unit length, or using $\langle v,u_i\rangle/\|v\|$ as the coefficient.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Parseval for $(3,4)$ in the standard basis of $\mathbb{R}^2$.

**Solution.** $9+16=25=\|(3,4)\|^2$.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Can four orthonormal vectors exist in $\mathbb{R}^3$?

**Solution.** No.
