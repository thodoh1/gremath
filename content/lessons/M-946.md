# Lesson M-946 — Analytic/holomorphic functions

Part W. W. Complex Analysis · **Complex functions** · Medium

Prerequisites: `D.1-O.4`.


This lesson isolates **Analytic/holomorphic functions** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a Medium skill: it appears less often, but when it appears it is often a discriminator. Complex analysis is additional topics: Cauchy–Riemann, residues at a basic level.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Analytic/holomorphic functions**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 3. Precise statement

$f$ is holomorphic (analytic) on an open set $U$ if it is complex-differentiable at every point of $U$. Holomorphic functions are automatically $C^\infty$ and equal their Taylor series in disks. Identity theorem: if $U$ is connected and $f=0$ on a set with a limit point in $U$, then $f\equiv 0$ on $U$. Entire means holomorphic on all of $\mathbb{C}$.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Is $e^z$ entire?

**Solution.** Yes; termwise derivative of $\sum z^n/n!$ is itself.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** $1/z$ is holomorphic on

**Solution.** $\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}$, not entire.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Calling a function holomorphic at a point because the difference quotient exists only there (e.g. $|z|^2$ at $0$ is not holomorphic on any open set).
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** If two entire functions agree on $\mathbb{Z}$, must they agree on $\mathbb{C}$?

**Solution.** No: $\sin(\pi z)$ vanishes on $\mathbb{Z}$ but is not identically $0$. The identity theorem needs a limit point in the domain; $\mathbb{Z}$ has none in $\mathbb{C}$.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Solution.** holomorphic.
