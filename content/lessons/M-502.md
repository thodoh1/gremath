## 3. Precise statement

A prime $p>1$ has no positive divisors other than $1$ and $p$. An integer $n>1$ is composite otherwise. $1$ is neither. $a,b$ are coprime (relatively prime) iff $\gcd(a,b)=1$, even if both are composite. Euclid: if $p\mid ab$ then $p\mid a$ or $p\mid b$. Infinitely many primes.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Is $91$ prime? Are $8$ and $15$ coprime?

**Solution.** $91=7\cdot 13$, composite. $\gcd(8,15)=1$, so coprime (neither need be prime).

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** If a prime $p$ divides $n!+1$ for $n\ge 2$, then $p$ is

**Solution.** larger than $n$ (else $p\mid n!$ so $p\mid 1$).

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Calling $1$ prime, or thinking coprime means ‘both prime’.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Smallest prime dividing $51$.

**Solution.** $3$, since $51=3\cdot 17$.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Are $14$ and $15$ coprime?

**Solution.** Yes, $\gcd=1$.
