# Lesson M-611 — Generators

Part N. N. Rings, Modules & Fields · **Modules** · Medium

Prerequisites: `N.1`.


This lesson isolates **Generators** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a Medium skill: it appears less often, but when it appears it is often a discriminator. Rings and fields appear as recognition and finite examples, especially $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ and $F[x]$.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Generators**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Modules*

Modules are vector spaces over rings. Submodules, quotients, homomorphisms, generators, free modules. When the ring is a field, you recover linear algebra.

### This skill, specifically

Modules are vector spaces over rings.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

A set $S\subseteq M$ generates $M$ if the smallest submodule containing $S$ is $M$: every $m$ is a finite $R$-linear combination of elements of $S$. $M$ is cyclic if one element generates it: $M\cong R/I$ for $I=\mathrm{Ann}(m)$. Finitely generated: some finite $S$. $\mathbb{Q}$ is not finitely generated as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module. Nakayama concerns generation after reducing modulo the Jacobson radical.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Show $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ is cyclic as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module. Does $\{(2,0),(0,3)\}$ generate $\mathbb{Z}^2$?

**Solution.** Yes, generated by $[1]$. No: $\{(2,0),(0,3)\}$ generates $2\mathbb{Z}\times 3\mathbb{Z}$, index $6$ in $\mathbb{Z}^2$. The standard basis $\{(1,0),(0,1)\}$ generates $\mathbb{Z}^2$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** Cyclic $\mathbb{Z}$-modules up to iso

**Solution.** $\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Confusing generating as a module with generating as a ring (or as a group under multiplication).
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Does $\{2,3\}$ generate $\mathbb{Z}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module?

**Solution.** Yes: $3-2=1$.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Is $\mathbb{Q}$ a cyclic $\mathbb{Z}$-module?

**Solution.** No: $1$ generates only $\mathbb{Z}$; any $p/q$ generates $\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{Z}$ at best a cyclic group not all of $\mathbb{Q}$.

## 9. Lock it in

You own this skill when you can do Drill 1 and Drill 2 tomorrow, mixed among unrelated problems, in under five minutes each, with the traps named in Section 7 avoided on purpose.
