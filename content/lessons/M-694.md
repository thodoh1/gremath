# Lesson M-694 — Heine-Borel theorem

Part P. P. Topology · **Separation & compactness** · Medium

Prerequisites: `P.1-P.2`.


This lesson isolates **Heine-Borel theorem** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a Medium skill: it appears less often, but when it appears it is often a discriminator. Topology appears as compactness, connectedness, open/closed, Heine–Borel.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Heine-Borel theorem**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Separation & compactness*

Hausdorff: points can be separated by opens. Compact: every open cover has a finite subcover. Continuous images of compact sets are compact. In $\mathbb{R}^n$, compact $\Leftrightarrow$ closed and bounded (Heine–Borel).

### This skill, specifically

Hausdorff: points can be separated by opens.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

Heine--Borel: a subset $K\subset\mathbb{R}^n$ is compact iff it is closed and bounded. Boundedness without closedness fails ($(0,1]$); closedness without boundedness fails ($\mathbb{Z}$). The theorem fails in infinite-dimensional normed spaces: the closed unit ball is not compact.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Which of $[0,1]$, $(0,1]$, $\mathbb{Z}$, $\{(x,y):x^2+y^2\le 1\}$ are compact in Euclidean space?

**Solution.** $[0,1]$ and the closed unit disk: closed and bounded. $(0,1]$ not closed. $\mathbb{Z}$ unbounded.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** Closed and bounded in $\mathbb{R}^n$ implies

**Solution.** compact.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Using Heine--Borel in $\mathbb{Q}$ or in an infinite-dimensional space.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Is $\overline{B}(0,1)$ compact in $\ell^2$?

**Solution.** No: the standard basis vectors have no convergent subsequence.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** $\{1/n\}\cup\{0\}$ compact in $\mathbb{R}$?

**Solution.** Yes: closed and bounded.
