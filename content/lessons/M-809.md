## 3. Precise statement

Breadth-first search (BFS) from $s$ explores vertices in order of increasing distance from $s$. The BFS layers are $\{v:d(s,v)=k\}$. On an unweighted graph, BFS computes shortest-path distances. Queue implementation: $O(|V|+|E|)$.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Star $K_{1,6}$ with BFS from a leaf. Distances of the center and of the other leaves?

**Solution.** Center at distance $1$; other leaves at distance $2$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** BFS from a vertex of $C_6$. Layer sizes?

**Solution.** $1,2,2,1$ (distances $0,1,2,3$).

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Using BFS to compute shortest paths on a graph with unequal positive edge weights (use Dijkstra).
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** In a connected unweighted graph, BFS tree edges vs non-tree edges: a non-tree edge can join

**Solution.** the same layer or adjacent layers, never skip a layer.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Runtime on a simple graph with $n=50$, $e=200$, BFS is $\Theta($what$)$?

**Solution.** $\Theta(n+e)=\Theta(250)$.
