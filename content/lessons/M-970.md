# Lesson M-970 — Floating-point arithmetic awareness

Part X. X. Numerical Analysis · **Numerical error** · Medium

This lesson isolates **Floating-point arithmetic awareness** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a Medium skill: it appears less often, but when it appears it is often a discriminator. Numerical methods are additional topics: Newton, error, quadrature recognition.

## 3. Precise statement

Floating-point numbers are typically stored as $\pm(1+m)2^{e}$ with a finite mantissa $m$ and exponent $e$ in a fixed range (IEEE-754 binary64: 52 mantissa bits, $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}=2^{-52}\approx 2.2\times 10^{-16}$). Overflow/underflow occur outside the exponent range. Not every real is representable; rounding maps to a nearby float. Guard digits and fused multiply-add affect error.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Why is $0.1+0.2$ not exactly $0.3$ in binary floating point?

**Solution.** $1/10$ is not a finite binary fraction, so each of $0.1,0.2,0.3$ is rounded; the rounded sum need not equal the rounded target.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** A typical double-precision $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}$ is about

**Solution.** $2\times 10^{-16}$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Thinking every decimal with two digits after the point is exactly representable in binary, or treating overflow as ‘infinity of the reals’ in algebra identities.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

**Drill 1.** Largest finite IEEE-754 binary32 magnitude is about
