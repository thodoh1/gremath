# Lesson M-634 — Epsilon-N definition

Part O. O. Real Analysis · **Sequences** · High

Prerequisites: `G.1`.


This lesson isolates **Epsilon-N definition** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a High skill: it is standard GRE material. You should be able to do it without warming up. Real analysis items are often ‘which statement is true?’ definition checks inside additional topics.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Epsilon-N definition**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 3. Precise statement

Epsilon-$N$ definition: $a_n\to L$ iff for every $\varepsilon>0$ there exists $N\in\mathbb{N}$ such that $n\ge N$ implies $|a_n-L|<\varepsilon$. The $N$ may depend on $\varepsilon$ (and on the sequence), not on a particular $n$.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Prove $1/n\to 0$ from the definition.

**Solution.** Let $\varepsilon>0$. Choose integer $N>1/\varepsilon$. Then $n\ge N$ implies $|1/n|<\varepsilon$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** To have $|a_n-2|<10^{-3}$ eventually, one must produce

**Solution.** some $N$ working for this $\varepsilon=10^{-3}$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Taking $N$ depending on $n$, or fixing one $\varepsilon$ and claiming the limit.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Does $N$ have to be the smallest possible?

**Solution.** No: any sufficiently large $N$ works.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Show $n/(n+1)\to 1$: $|n/(n+1)-1|=1/(n+1)<\varepsilon$ when

**Solution.** $n+1>1/\varepsilon$.

## 9. Lock it in

You own this skill when you can do Drill 1 and Drill 2 tomorrow, mixed among unrelated problems, in under five minutes each, with the traps named in Section 7 avoided on purpose.
