# Lesson M-612 — Free modules

Part N. N. Rings, Modules & Fields · **Modules** · Medium

Prerequisites: `N.1`.


This lesson isolates **Free modules** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a Medium skill: it appears less often, but when it appears it is often a discriminator. Rings and fields appear as recognition and finite examples, especially $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ and $F[x]$.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Free modules**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Modules*

Modules are vector spaces over rings. Submodules, quotients, homomorphisms, generators, free modules. When the ring is a field, you recover linear algebra.

### This skill, specifically

Modules are vector spaces over rings.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

A free $R$-module on a set $S$ is isomorphic to $\bigoplus_{s\in S} R$, with basis $S$: every element is a unique finite $R$-linear combination of basis elements. $R^n$ is free of rank $n$. Over a commutative ring, rank is well-defined. $\mathbb{Z}/n$ is not free over $\mathbb{Z}$. Submodules of free modules over a PID are free of smaller or equal rank. Bases exist for vector spaces; not for general modules (e.g. $\mathbb{Q}$ over $\mathbb{Z}$).

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Is $\mathbb{Z}^2$ free? Is $2\mathbb{Z}$ free over $\mathbb{Z}$? Is $\mathbb{Z}/2$ free over $\mathbb{Z}$?

**Solution.** Yes, standard basis. Yes: $\{2\}$ is a basis (unique writing $n\cdot 2$). No: any generating set is linearly dependent over $\mathbb{Z}$ because $2\cdot[1]=0$, or rank would imply a submodule of $\mathbb{Z}^k$ isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}/2$, impossible in a torsion-free module.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** Rank of $R^n$ as an $R$-module

**Solution.** $n$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Calling every finitely generated module free (false: torsion modules).
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** A basis of $\mathbb{Z}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module.

**Solution.** $\{1\}$, or $\{-1\}$.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Is $\mathbb{Q}$ free over $\mathbb{Z}$?

**Solution.** No: any two rationals are $\mathbb{Z}$-linearly dependent, but one element does not span.
