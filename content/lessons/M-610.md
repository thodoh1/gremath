# Lesson M-610 — Module homomorphisms

Part N. N. Rings, Modules & Fields · **Modules** · Medium

Prerequisites: `N.1`.


This lesson isolates **Module homomorphisms** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a Medium skill: it appears less often, but when it appears it is often a discriminator. Rings and fields appear as recognition and finite examples, especially $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ and $F[x]$.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Module homomorphisms**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Modules*

Modules are vector spaces over rings. Submodules, quotients, homomorphisms, generators, free modules. When the ring is a field, you recover linear algebra.

### This skill, specifically

Modules are vector spaces over rings.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

An $R$-module homomorphism $\phi:M\to N$ is an additive group hom with $\phi(rm)=r\phi(m)$. Kernel is a submodule of $M$, image a submodule of $N$. First iso: $M/\ker\cong\mathrm{im}$. $\mathrm{Hom}_R(R,M)\cong M$ via $\phi\mapsto\phi(1)$. $\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}/n,\mathbb{Z}/m)\cong\mathbb{Z}/\gcd(n,m)$.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Describe $\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}/2,\mathbb{Z}/4)$ and a nontrivial map.

**Solution.** $\cong\mathbb{Z}/2$. A generator $1\bmod 2$ may go to an element of order dividing $2$ in $\mathbb{Z}/4$, i.e. $0$ or $2$. Nontrivial: $1\mapsto 2$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** $\mathrm{Hom}_F(F^n,F^m)$ is

**Solution.** $m\times n$ matrices, $F$-linear maps.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Checking additivity but not $R$-linearity (over $\mathbb{Z}$ they coincide; over $\mathbb{R}$ a group hom of $(\mathbb{R},+)$ need not be $\mathbb{R}$-linear without extra hypotheses).
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Kernel of $M\to M/N$, $m\mapsto m+N$.

**Solution.** $N$.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Is $\phi:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}$, $\phi(n)=2n$, $\mathbb{Z}$-linear?

**Solution.** Yes, and injective, not surjective.

## 9. Lock it in

You own this skill when you can do Drill 1 and Drill 2 tomorrow, mixed among unrelated problems, in under five minutes each, with the traps named in Section 7 avoided on purpose.
