# Lesson M-256 — Using expansions to evaluate limits

Part E. E. Calculus I — Limits & Differentiation · **Taylor theorem & approximation** · High

Prerequisites: `E.4`.


This lesson isolates **Using expansions to evaluate limits** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a High skill: it is standard GRE material. You should be able to do it without warming up. This is the core of the calculus half of the exam. Limits, derivatives, and applications are everywhere.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Using expansions to evaluate limits**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Taylor theorem & approximation*

Taylor polynomials are the unique polynomials that match $f$ and its first $n$ derivatives at a point. Remainder estimates tell you the error. Big-O notation tracks the order. Limits that look like L’Hôpital often die faster with two terms of Taylor.

### This skill, specifically

Taylor polynomials are the unique polynomials that match $f$ and its first $n$ derivatives at a point.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

To evaluate a limit, replace each function by its Taylor expansion to the first order that does not cancel, then simplify. This is L'Hôpital with bookkeeping. Standard replacements: $\sin x=x-x^3/6+O(x^5)$, $e^x=1+x+x^2/2+O(x^3)$, $\ln(1+x)=x-x^2/2+O(x^3)$.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** $\lim_{x\to 0}\dfrac{e^x-1-x}{x^2}$.

**Solution.** $e^x=1+x+x^2/2+O(x^3)$, numerator $x^2/2+O(x^3)$, limit $1/2$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** $\lim_{x\to 0}\dfrac{1-\cos x}{x\sin x}$

**Solution.** $(x^2/2+O(x^4))/(x\cdot x+O(x^3))=1/2$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Expanding numerator to order $1$ when the first two orders cancel.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** $\lim_{x\to 0}\dfrac{\tan x-x}{x^3}$.

**Solution.** $1/3$ (use $\tan x=x+x^3/3+O(x^5)$).

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Why is Taylor often faster than three L'Hôpitals?

**Solution.** You see the first surviving degree in one line.
