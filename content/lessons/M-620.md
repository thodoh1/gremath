# Lesson M-620 — Minimal polynomial

Part N. N. Rings, Modules & Fields · **Fields & extensions** · Medium

Prerequisites: `N.3-L.2`.


This lesson isolates **Minimal polynomial** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a Medium skill: it appears less often, but when it appears it is often a discriminator. Rings and fields appear as recognition and finite examples, especially $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ and $F[x]$.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Minimal polynomial**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Fields & extensions*

A field has every nonzero element invertible. Extensions $K/F$ have degree $[K:F]=\dim_F K$. Algebraic vs transcendental, minimal polynomials, splitting fields, $\mathbb{F}_p$ and $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$.

### This skill, specifically

A field has every nonzero element invertible.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

The minimal polynomial $m_{\alpha,F}$ of an algebraic $\alpha$ over $F$ is the unique monic generator of the kernel of $F[x]\to K$, $f\mapsto f(\alpha)$. It is monic irreducible, and it divides every polynomial in $F[x]$ that vanishes at $\alpha$. Degree of $\alpha$ is $\deg m_\alpha$. Changing $F$ can drop the degree: $m_{i,\mathbb{Q}}=x^2+1$, $m_{i,\mathbb{R}}=x^2+1$, $m_{i,\mathbb{C}}=x-i$.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Minimal polynomial of $\sqrt{2}+\sqrt{3}$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, and of $\sqrt{2}$ over $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$.

**Solution.** $x^4-10x^2+1$ is monic of degree $4=[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2},\sqrt{3}):\mathbb{Q}]$ and $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}+\sqrt{3})=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2},\sqrt{3})$, hence it is the min poly. Over $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$, $\sqrt{2}$ has min poly $x-\sqrt{2}$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** Min poly of $e^{2\pi i/3}$ over $\mathbb{Q}$

**Solution.** $x^2+x+1$ (cyclotomic $\Phi_3$).

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Taking a polynomial that $\alpha$ satisfies without making it monic or irreducible (e.g. $x^2-2$ is min poly of $\sqrt{2}$, not $2x^2-4$).
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Min poly of $2\sqrt{2}$ over $\mathbb{Q}$.

**Solution.** $x^2-8$.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Does $x^2-2$ remain the min poly of $\sqrt{2}$ over $\mathbb{R}$?

**Solution.** No: over $\mathbb{R}$ it splits, min poly is $x-\sqrt{2}$.
