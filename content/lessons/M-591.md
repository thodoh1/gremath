# Lesson M-591 — Ideal definition

Part N. N. Rings, Modules & Fields · **Ideals & quotient rings** · Medium

Prerequisites: `N.1`.


This lesson isolates **Ideal definition** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a Medium skill: it appears less often, but when it appears it is often a discriminator. Rings and fields appear as recognition and finite examples, especially $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ and $F[x]$.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Ideal definition**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Ideals & quotient rings*

Ideals are kernels of ring maps. Principal, prime, maximal. $R/I$ is a field iff $I$ is maximal (in commutative unital rings). Polynomial ideals in $F[x]$ are principal.

### This skill, specifically

Ideals are kernels of ring maps.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

An (two-sided) ideal $I\subseteq R$ is an additive subgroup with $r\in R$, $a\in I\Rightarrow ra,ar\in I$ (absorbs multiplication from $R$). For commutative unital $R$ it is enough: $0\in I$, closed under addition, and $R I\subseteq I$. Examples: $n\mathbb{Z}$ in $\mathbb{Z}$; $(f)$ in $F[x]$; $\{0\}$ and $R$. Ideals of $\mathbb{Z}$ are exactly $n\mathbb{Z}$. The kernel of a ring hom is an ideal.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Show $I=\{f\in\mathbb{R}[x]:f(2)=f'(2)=0\}$ is an ideal. Is $2\mathbb{Z}$ an ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$?

**Solution.** $I=(x-2)^2$, an ideal. Yes, $2\mathbb{Z}=(2)$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** Are all additive subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}$ ideals?

**Solution.** Yes, they are all $n\mathbb{Z}$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Checking $I\cdot I\subseteq I$ only (that is a subring test ingredient, not absorption by $R$).
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Is $M_2(2\mathbb{Z})$ an ideal of $M_2(\mathbb{Z})$?

**Solution.** Yes: multiplying a matrix of even entries by any integer matrix keeps even entries.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** The units of $R$ lie in an ideal $I$ only if

**Solution.** $I=R$ (because $1\in I$ then).
