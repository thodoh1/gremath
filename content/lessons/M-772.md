# Lesson M-772 — Double counting

Part R. R. Combinatorics · **Combinatorial identities** · High

Prerequisites: `R.1`.


This lesson isolates **Double counting** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a High skill: it is standard GRE material. You should be able to do it without warming up. Combinatorics is additional topics and also probability setup.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Double counting**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Combinatorial identities*

Pascal, binomial theorem, stars and bars, inclusion-exclusion, pigeonhole, double counting, bijections, recurrences. Prove by counting two ways, not only by algebra.

### This skill, specifically

Pascal, binomial theorem, stars and bars, inclusion-exclusion, pigeonhole, double counting, bijections, recurrences.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

Double counting: evaluate the same finite set of pairs (or flags) in two ways. The equality of the two expressions is an identity. Prototype: count pairs $(v,e)$ with $v$ incident to $e$ to get $\sum_v\deg v=2|E|$.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Count pairs (committee of $2$ from $n$ people, chair of that committee) two ways.

**Solution.** Choose the pair then the chair: $\binom{n}{2}\cdot 2$. Choose the chair then the other member: $n(n-1)$. Hence $n(n-1)=n(n-1)$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** Number of edges in $K_7$ by double counting degrees.

**Solution.** Each of $7$ vertices has degree $6$, sum $42$, so $|E|=21=\binom{7}{2}$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Counting ordered pairs on one side and unordered pairs on the other without converting.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** How many ways to choose a $3$-subset of $[10]$ and then an element of it?

**Solution.** $\binom{10}{3}\cdot 3=360= \binom{9}{2}\cdot 10$, since pick the element first ($10$) then $2$ companions from $9$.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Prove $\sum_{k} k\binom{n}{k}=n 2^{n-1}$ by double counting.

**Solution.** Left: choose a marked $k$-subset. Right: pick the marked element ($n$) then any subset of the rest ($2^{n-1}$).
