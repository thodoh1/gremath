# Lesson M-618 — Algebraic element

Part N. N. Rings, Modules & Fields · **Fields & extensions** · Medium

Prerequisites: `N.3-L.2`.


This lesson isolates **Algebraic element** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a Medium skill: it appears less often, but when it appears it is often a discriminator. Rings and fields appear as recognition and finite examples, especially $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ and $F[x]$.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Algebraic element**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Fields & extensions*

A field has every nonzero element invertible. Extensions $K/F$ have degree $[K:F]=\dim_F K$. Algebraic vs transcendental, minimal polynomials, splitting fields, $\mathbb{F}_p$ and $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$.

### This skill, specifically

A field has every nonzero element invertible.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

An element $\alpha\in K$ is algebraic over $F$ if $f(\alpha)=0$ for some nonzero $f\in F[x]$. Then there is a unique monic polynomial of least degree with this property, the minimal polynomial $m_\alpha$, which is irreducible, and $F(\alpha)\cong F[x]/(m_\alpha)$ with $[F(\alpha):F]=\deg m_\alpha$. A finite extension is algebraic. Sums and products of algebraic elements are algebraic.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Show $\sqrt{2}+\sqrt{3}$ is algebraic over $\mathbb{Q}$ by producing a polynomial.

**Solution.** Let $\alpha=\sqrt{2}+\sqrt{3}$. $\alpha^2=5+2\sqrt{6}$, $(\alpha^2-5)^2=24$, $\alpha^4-10\alpha^2+1=0$. So algebraic, degree dividing $4$ (in fact $4$).

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** $\sqrt[4]{2}$ is algebraic over $\mathbb{Q}$ of degree

**Solution.** $4$: min poly $x^4-2$ (Eisenstein $p=2$).

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Producing a polynomial that $\alpha$ satisfies and calling its degree the degree of $\alpha$ without proving irreducibility.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Is $i$ algebraic over $\mathbb{R}$?

**Solution.** Yes, $x^2+1$. Degree $1$ over $\mathbb{C}$, $2$ over $\mathbb{R}$.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Algebraic over $\mathbb{Q}$: $e^{2\pi i/5}$.

**Solution.** Yes, a root of the $5$th cyclotomic polynomial.
