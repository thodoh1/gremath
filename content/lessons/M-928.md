# Lesson M-928 — Line-plane relationships

Part V. V. Geometry · **3D geometry** · High

This lesson isolates **Line-plane relationships** and trains it until it is automatic.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Line-plane relationships**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 3. Precise statement

A line is parallel to a plane if its direction $\mathbf{d}$ is orthogonal to the plane’s normal $\mathbf{n}$ ($\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0$) and the line does not lie in the plane; it lies in the plane if additionally one point of the line satisfies the plane equation. The line is perpendicular to the plane if $\mathbf{d}$ is parallel to $\mathbf{n}$. Two planes are parallel iff normals are parallel; perpendicular iff $\mathbf{n}_1\cdot\mathbf{n}_2=0$.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Line $(1,0,0)+t(0,1,0)$ and plane $z=2$. Parallel, intersecting, or contained?

**Solution.** Parallel and not contained (direction $\perp$ normal $\mathbf{k}$, point has $z=0\neq 2$).

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** Planes $x+y+z=1$ and $x+y+z=5$ are

**Solution.** parallel and distinct.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Concluding a line is parallel to a plane from $\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0$ without checking whether it lies in the plane.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Is $x=y=z$ perpendicular to $x+y+z=0$?

**Solution.** Yes: direction $(1,1,1)$ is the normal.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Two planes with normals $(1,0,0)$ and $(0,1,0)$ meet at

**Solution.** a right dihedral angle (normals perpendicular).
