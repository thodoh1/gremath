# Lesson M-992 — Recognize when a statement is false by counterexample

Part Y. Y. Mixed GRE Problem-Solving · **Theorem/definition recognition** · Critical

Prerequisites: `A-Q`.


This lesson isolates **Recognize when a statement is false by counterexample** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a Critical skill: it is used constantly, and a hole here leaks points all over the paper. This is not content weight; it is how every weighted item is actually scored under a clock.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Recognize when a statement is false by counterexample**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 3. Precise statement

To disprove $\forall x\,P(x)$, one explicit counterexample suffices. The GRE loves universally quantified claims about continuous functions, sequences, matrices, and series. Construct the smallest counterexample: a $2\times 2$ matrix, a two-term sequence, $f(x)=x^2\sin(1/x)$, or the harmonic series. Do not try to prove a false universal statement by ‘generic’ computation.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Claim: every sequence of continuous functions on $[0,1]$ that converges pointwise has a continuous limit. Counterexample?

**Solution.** $f_n(x)=x^n$: pointwise limit $0$ on $[0,1)$ and $1$ at $1$, discontinuous.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** Claim: $\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=0\Rightarrow X,Y$ independent. Counterexample

**Solution.** $X\sim N(0,1)$, $Y=X^2$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Offering a true special case as a ‘counterexample’, or a non-counterexample that violates hidden extra hypotheses you added.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Claim: every $2\times 2$ real matrix with $\det=1$ is diagonalizable over $\mathbb{R}$. Counterexample

**Solution.** $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\ 0&1\end{pmatrix}$ (Jordan block; only eigenvalue $1$, not two independent eigenvectors).

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Claim: if $f'(c)=0$ then $c$ is a local extremum. Counterexample

**Solution.** $f(x)=x^3$ at $0$.

## 9. Lock it in

You own this skill when you can do Drill 1 and Drill 2 tomorrow, mixed among unrelated problems, in under five minutes each, with the traps named in Section 7 avoided on purpose.
