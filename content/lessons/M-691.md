# Lesson M-691 — Open-cover definition

Part P. P. Topology · **Separation & compactness** · Medium

Prerequisites: `P.1-P.2`.


This lesson isolates **Open-cover definition** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a Medium skill: it appears less often, but when it appears it is often a discriminator. Topology appears as compactness, connectedness, open/closed, Heine–Borel.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Open-cover definition**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Separation & compactness*

Hausdorff: points can be separated by opens. Compact: every open cover has a finite subcover. Continuous images of compact sets are compact. In $\mathbb{R}^n$, compact $\Leftrightarrow$ closed and bounded (Heine–Borel).

### This skill, specifically

Hausdorff: points can be separated by opens.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

An open cover of $X$ is a family of open sets whose union is $X$. Compactness is: every such family has a finite subcover. Equivalent for metric spaces: every sequence has a convergent subsequence; every infinite subset has a limit point; complete and totally bounded.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** The sets $U_n=(-n,n)$ cover $\mathbb{R}$. Why does this show $\mathbb{R}$ is not compact?

**Solution.** Any finite subcollection is contained in $(-N,N)$ for $N=\max n_i$, missing $|x|\ge N$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** A subcover is a

**Solution.** subcollection that still covers.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Using closed covers, or thinking one particular cover having a finite subcover is enough for compactness.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Cover $[0,1]$ by $\{( -1,1-1/n):n\ge 2\}\cup\{(1/2,2)\}$. Finite subcover?

**Solution.** Yes: the last interval plus one $U_n$ that reaches $1/2$; compactness guarantees some finite subcover for every cover.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Lebesgue number intuition: in a compact metric space, some $\delta>0$ has every set of diameter $<\delta$ inside a single set of the cover: true?

**Solution.** True.
