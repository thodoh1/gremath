This lesson isolates **Algorithmic complexity** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a Medium skill: it appears less often, but when it appears it is often a discriminator. Graph theory is additional topics: trees, Euler, handshaking, planarity.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Algorithmic complexity**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 3. Precise statement

Algorithmic complexity compares growth of time/space as a function of input size $n$ (or $|V|,|E|$). Worst-case $T(n)$ is the quantity of interest on GRE-style questions. Polynomial vs exponential is the practical divide: $n^3$ vs $2^n$. Graph algorithms are often $O(n+m)$ (linear in the representation).

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Which is asymptotically faster for large $n$: $\Theta(n^2)$ or $\Theta(n\log n)$?

**Solution.** $\Theta(n\log n)$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** Hamiltonian-cycle brute force on $n$ labeled vertices is about $n!$ checks. Stirling: $n!\sim$

**Solution.** $(n/e)^n\sqrt{2\pi n}$, super-exponential vs $2^n$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Calling an $O(n^2)$ algorithm `quadratic' then claiming it beats $O(2^n)$ for $n=3$ as if that settled asymptotics.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** BFS on an adjacency list is $O(n+m)$. On an adjacency matrix?

**Solution.** $O(n^2)$ even if $m$ is small, because you scan rows.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.
