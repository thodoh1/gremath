# Lesson M-550 — Cycle notation

Part M. M. Group Theory · **Permutation groups** · High

Prerequisites: `M.1-Q.3`.


This lesson isolates **Cycle notation** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a High skill: it is standard GRE material. You should be able to do it without warming up. Group theory is the main abstract-algebra GRE diet: orders, Lagrange, homomorphisms.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Cycle notation**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Permutation groups*

$S_n$ is the group of bijections on $n$ letters. Cycle decomposition, disjoint cycles commute, transpositions generate, sign homomorphism, $A_n=\ker(\mathrm{sign})$.

### This skill, specifically

$S_n$ is the group of bijections on $n$ letters.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

Cycle notation: $(a_1\,a_2\,\ldots\,a_k)$ is the permutation sending $a_i\mapsto a_{i+1}$, $a_k\mapsto a_1$, and fixing the rest. A $k$-cycle has order $k$. Cycles are unique up to rotation: $(1\,2\,3)=(2\,3\,1)$. $(a\,b)$ is a transposition. Disjoint cycle decomposition is unique up to order of the cycles.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Write $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2&3&4&5\\ 3&1&2&5&4\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ in cycle notation.

**Solution.** $1\mapsto 3\mapsto 2\mapsto 1$ and $4\mapsto 5\mapsto 4$, so $(1\,3\,2)(4\,5)$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** $(1\,2\,3)^{-1}$ equals

**Solution.** $(1\,3\,2)$ (reverse the cycle).

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Reading $(1\,2\,3)$ as swapping $1$ with $2$ and leaving $3$ (that is a transposition times a fixed point).
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Cycle type of $(1\,4\,2\,7)(3\,5)$ in $S_7$.

**Solution.** A $4$-cycle and a $2$-cycle (and a $1$-cycle $6$).

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Does $(1\,2\,3)=(3\,1\,2)$?

**Solution.** Yes, rotation. Not equal to $(1\,3\,2)$.

## 9. Lock it in

You own this skill when you can do Drill 1 and Drill 2 tomorrow, mixed among unrelated problems, in under five minutes each, with the traps named in Section 7 avoided on purpose.
