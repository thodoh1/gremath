# Lesson M-655 — Uniform continuity

Part O. O. Real Analysis · **Continuity** · High

Prerequisites: `E.2-O.2`.


This lesson isolates **Uniform continuity** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a High skill: it is standard GRE material. You should be able to do it without warming up. Real analysis items are often ‘which statement is true?’ definition checks inside additional topics.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Uniform continuity**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Continuity*

$f$ is continuous at $a$ when $\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=f(a)$. Types of discontinuity (removable, jump, infinite) are classified by one-sided limits. IVT and EVT are the two big existence theorems for continuous functions on closed intervals.

### This skill, specifically

$f$ is continuous at $a$ when $\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=f(a)$.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

$f$ is uniformly continuous on $S$ if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $\delta>0$ working simultaneously for all $x,y\in S$: $|x-y|<\delta\Rightarrow|f(x)-f(y)|<\varepsilon$. Heine--Cantor: continuous on a compact set $\Rightarrow$ uniformly continuous. Uniform continuity $\Rightarrow$ Cauchy sequences map to Cauchy sequences.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Is $f(x)=x^2$ uniformly continuous on $\mathbb{R}$? On $[0,1]$?

**Solution.** No on $\mathbb{R}$: $|x^2-y^2|=|x+y||x-y|$ becomes large for large $x$. Yes on $[0,1]$ by Heine--Cantor.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** Is $1/x$ uniformly continuous on $(0,1]$?

**Solution.** No: $x_n=1/n$, $y_n=1/(n+1)$ are close but $f$-values differ by about $1$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Confusing pointwise continuity (local $\delta(a,\varepsilon)$) with a single $\delta(\varepsilon)$.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Is $\sqrt{x}$ uniformly continuous on $[0,\infty)$?

**Solution.** Yes: $|\sqrt{x}-\sqrt{y}|\le\sqrt{|x-y|}$.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Continuous functions on open intervals need not be uniform: classic.

**Solution.** $1/x$ on $(0,1)$, or $x^2$ on $\mathbb{R}$.
