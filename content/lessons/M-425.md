# Lesson M-425 — Projection

Part K. K. Linear Algebra · **Vectors & geometry** · Critical

Prerequisites: `A.1`.


This lesson isolates **Projection** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a Critical skill: it is used constantly, and a hole here leaks points all over the paper. Linear algebra is a large share of the algebra portion. Rank, kernels, eigenvalues, and determinants are exam staples.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Projection**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Vectors & geometry*

### This skill, specifically

Vectors add, scale, dot (angles, projections), cross in $\mathbb{R}^3$ (area, orthogonality), triple product (volume).

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

The orthogonal projection of $\mathbf{u}$ onto nonzero $\mathbf{v}$ is $\mathrm{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u}=\dfrac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{v}$. The remainder $\mathbf{u}-\mathrm{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u}$ is orthogonal to $\mathbf{v}$. Scalar projection is $\dfrac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{|\mathbf{v}|}$.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Project $(2,3)$ onto $(1,1)$.

**Solution.** $\dfrac{5}{2}(1,1)=\bigl(\frac52,\frac52\bigr)$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** $\mathrm{proj}_{(1,0)}(3,4)$ equals

**Solution.** $(3,0)$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Using $\dfrac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{|\mathbf{u}|^2}\mathbf{u}$ (projecting $\mathbf{v}$ onto $\mathbf{u}$ instead), or forgetting to multiply back by $\mathbf{v}$.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.
