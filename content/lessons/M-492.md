# Lesson M-492 — Orthogonal projection

Part K. K. Linear Algebra · **Inner-product spaces** · High

Prerequisites: `K.1-K.7`.


This lesson isolates **Orthogonal projection** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a High skill: it is standard GRE material. You should be able to do it without warming up. Linear algebra is a large share of the algebra portion. Rank, kernels, eigenvalues, and determinants are exam staples.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Orthogonal projection**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Inner-product spaces*

Inner products give geometry: orthogonality, norms, projections, Gram–Schmidt, least squares. Symmetric real matrices are orthogonally diagonalizable (spectral theorem). SVD is the rectangular cousin.

### This skill, specifically

## 3. Precise statement

The orthogonal projection of $v$ onto a subspace $W$ with orthonormal basis $\{u_i\}$ is $\mathrm{proj}_W v=\sum\langle v,u_i\rangle u_i$. It is the unique closest point of $W$ to $v$: $\|v-\mathrm{proj}_W v\|=\mathrm{dist}(v,W)$, and $v-\mathrm{proj}_W v\in W^\perp$. For a general basis, solve the normal equations.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Project $(3,4)$ onto $\mathrm{span}\{(1,0)\}$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$.

**Solution.** $(3,0)$. Distance $4$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** If $v\in W$, $\mathrm{proj}_W v$ equals

**Solution.** $v$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Summing $\langle v,w_i\rangle w_i$ over a non-orthonormal basis of $W$ (must use the dual / normal equations).
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.
