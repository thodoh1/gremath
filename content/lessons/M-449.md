# Lesson M-449 — Span

Part K. K. Linear Algebra · **Vector spaces** · Critical

Prerequisites: `K.1-K.3`.


This lesson isolates **Span** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a Critical skill: it is used constantly, and a hole here leaks points all over the paper. Linear algebra is a large share of the algebra portion. Rank, kernels, eigenvalues, and determinants are exam staples.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Span**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 3. Precise statement

The span of $S=\{\mathbf{v}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{v}_k\}$ is the set of all linear combinations $\sum c_i\mathbf{v}_i$. It is the smallest subspace containing $S$. $\mathrm{span}\,S=V$ means $S$ generates $V$. Adding a vector already in the span does not enlarge it.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** $\mathrm{span}\{(1,2),(2,4)\}$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$, versus $\mathrm{span}\{(1,0),(0,1)\}$.

**Solution.** The line through $(1,2)$; all of $\mathbb{R}^2$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** Does $(1,1,1)$ lie in $\mathrm{span}\{(1,0,1),(0,1,0)\}$?

**Solution.** Yes: $1\cdot(1,0,1)+1\cdot(0,1,0)$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Writing $\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{v}\}$ as two opposite rays and omitting the line, or requiring coefficients $c_i\ge 0$ (that is a cone, not span).
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.
