This lesson isolates **Taylor remainder concept** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a High skill: it is standard GRE material. You should be able to do it without warming up. This is the core of the calculus half of the exam. Limits, derivatives, and applications are everywhere.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Taylor remainder concept**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Taylor theorem & approximation*

Taylor polynomials are the unique polynomials that match $f$ and its first $n$ derivatives at a point. Remainder estimates tell you the error. Big-O notation tracks the order. Limits that look like L’Hôpital often die faster with two terms of Taylor.

### This skill, specifically

Taylor polynomials are the unique polynomials that match $f$ and its first $n$ derivatives at a point.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

Lagrange remainder: $f(x)=P_n(x)+R_n(x)$ with $R_n(x)=\dfrac{f^{(n+1)}(c)}{(n+1)!}(x-a)^{n+1}$ for some $c$ between $a$ and $x$. Bounding $|f^{(n+1)}|$ bounds the error.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Bound the error of $P_1$ for $e^x$ at $0$ on $[0,1]$.

**Solution.** $R_1=e^c x^2/2$, $|R_1|\le e/2$ on $[0,1]$. Sharper: $e^x\le e$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Solution.** $M|x-a|^{n+1}/(n+1)!$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Thinking the remainder is the next Taylor term exactly (it is that term at a mystery $c$).
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Does $R_n(a)=0$?

**Solution.** Yes.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** For $\sin x$, $|R_3(x)|$ near $0$ is $O($?

**Solution.** $|x|^5$ because the degree-$4$ term vanishes too; Lagrange with $n=3$ gives $O(x^4)$, but the next nonzero is $x^5$.
