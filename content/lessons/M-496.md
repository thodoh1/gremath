## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Inner-product spaces*

Inner products give geometry: orthogonality, norms, projections, Gram–Schmidt, least squares. Symmetric real matrices are orthogonally diagonalizable (spectral theorem). SVD is the rectangular cousin.

## 3. Precise statement

A real quadratic form is $q(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^T A\mathbf{x}$ with $A$ symmetric (replace a general matrix by its symmetric part). Completing the square, or substituting $\mathbf{x}=Q\mathbf{y}$ from the spectral theorem, yields $q=\sum\lambda_i y_i^2$ (principal-axis theorem). $q$ is positive definite iff every $\lambda_i>0$.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** $A=\begin{pmatrix}4&-1\\-1&4\end{pmatrix}$. Write $q(x,y)$ and classify.

**Solution.** $q=4x^2-2xy+4y^2$. Eigenvalues $5,3>0$: positive definite.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** The cross term $2bxy$ in $ax^2+2bxy+cy^2$ comes from

**Solution.** off-diagonal $A_{12}=b$.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Using a non-symmetric $A$ in $\mathbf{x}^T A\mathbf{x}$ without symmetrizing, then reading eigenvalues of that non-symmetric matrix as the inertia of $q$.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Signature of $x^2-y^2$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$.

**Solution.** $(1,1)$: one positive, one negative eigenvalue.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** $q$ positive semidefinite iff

**Solution.** every eigenvalue $\ge 0$.
