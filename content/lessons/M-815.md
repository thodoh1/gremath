## 3. Precise statement

Euclidean algorithm: $\gcd(a,b)=\gcd(b,a\bmod b)$, and $\gcd(a,0)=a$. It is the model of a fast recursive/iterative number-theoretic algorithm: $O(\log\min(a,b))$ steps (Lamé: worst case Fibonacci). Bézout: it also yields $ax+by=\gcd$.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Compute $\gcd(252,198)$ by Euclid.

**Solution.** $252=1\cdot 198+54$, $198=3\cdot 54+36$, $54=1\cdot 36+18$, $36=2\cdot 18+0$, so $\gcd=18$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** $\gcd(1071,462)$.

**Solution.** $1071=2\cdot 462+147$, $462=3\cdot 147+21$, $147=7\cdot 21+0$, so $21$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Stopping at the first remainder instead of at remainder $0$, or using $\gcd(a,b)=ab$ (that is $\mathrm{lcm}\cdot\gcd=ab$).
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** $\gcd(54,17)$. How many divisions until $0$?

**Solution.** $54=3\cdot 17+3$, $17=5\cdot 3+2$, $3=1\cdot 2+1$, $2=2\cdot 1+0$: gcd $1$, four division steps.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Why is Euclid faster than trial division up to $\sqrt{n}$?

**Solution.** Remainders shrink exponentially (at worst like Fibonacci), not like $\sqrt{n}$ trials.
