# Lesson M-564 — Group homomorphism

Part M. M. Group Theory · **Homomorphisms & quotients** · High

Prerequisites: `M.3`.


This lesson isolates **Group homomorphism** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a High skill: it is standard GRE material. You should be able to do it without warming up. Group theory is the main abstract-algebra GRE diet: orders, Lagrange, homomorphisms.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Group homomorphism**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Homomorphisms & quotients*

A homomorphism preserves the operation. Kernel is a normal subgroup. First isomorphism theorem: $G/\ker\phi\cong\mathrm{im}\,\phi$. Quotients exist precisely because of normality.

### This skill, specifically

A homomorphism preserves the operation.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

A group homomorphism is a map $\phi:G\to K$ with $\phi(ab)=\phi(a)\phi(b)$. Then $\phi(e_G)=e_K$ and $\phi(a^{-1})=\phi(a)^{-1}$. Examples: $\det:GL_n\to F^\times$, $\mathrm{sgn}:S_n\to\{\pm 1\}$, $\exp:(\mathbb{R},+)\to(\mathbb{R}_{>0},\cdot)$, reduction $\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, inclusion of a subgroup. Homomorphisms preserve orders of elements in the sense $\mathrm{ord}(\phi(g))\mid\mathrm{ord}(g)$.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Show $\phi:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}$ given by $\phi(n)=2n$ is a homomorphism. Is $\det:GL_2(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}^\times$ a homomorphism?

**Solution.** $\phi(n+m)=2(n+m)=2n+2m$. Yes $\det(AB)=\det A\det B$ with kernel $SL_2$. (On all of $M_2$ the same identity holds, but $M_2$ is not a multiplicative group.)

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** $\phi:G\to K$ a hom. $\phi(g^k)$ equals

**Solution.** $\phi(g)^k$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Requiring $\phi(ab)=\phi(b)\phi(a)$ (that is an anti-homomorphism) or forgetting to check the identity.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Is $x\mapsto |x|$ a hom $(\mathbb{R}^\times,\cdot)\to(\mathbb{R}_{>0},\cdot)$?

**Solution.** Yes: $|xy|=|x||y|$.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** The zero map $\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}$, $n\mapsto 0$: a hom of additive groups?

**Solution.** Yes.

You own this skill when you can do Drill 1 and Drill 2 tomorrow, mixed among unrelated problems, in under five minutes each, with the traps named in Section 7 avoided on purpose.
