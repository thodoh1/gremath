# Lesson M-938 — Dot product and angle

Part V. V. Geometry · **Analytic/vector geometry** · High

Prerequisites: `K.1-C.5`.


This lesson isolates **Dot product and angle** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a High skill: it is standard GRE material. You should be able to do it without warming up. Geometry is additional topics plus analytic geometry inside calculus/algebra items.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Dot product and angle**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 3. Precise statement

The dot product $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=\sum u_i v_i=\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|\cos\theta$ with $\theta$ the angle between them. Orthogonal $\Leftrightarrow$ $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=0$. Cauchy–Schwarz: $|\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}|\le\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|$. In $\mathbb{R}^2$, the signed angle satisfies $\cos\theta=\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}/(\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|)$.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Angle between $(1,0,0)$ and $(1,1,0)$.

**Solution.** $\cos\theta=1/\sqrt{2}$, so $\theta=\pi/4$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** $(1,2,3)\cdot(1,-1,1)=$

**Solution.** $2$. Not orthogonal.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Using $\cos\theta=\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}$ without dividing by the norms, or confusing orthogonal with parallel.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Are $(1,1,1)$ and $(1,-2,1)$ orthogonal?

**Solution.** Yes: $1-2+1=0$.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{u}=$

**Solution.** $\|\mathbf{u}\|^2$.
