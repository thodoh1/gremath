# Lesson M-937 — Vector representation of lines/planes

Part V. V. Geometry · **Analytic/vector geometry** · High

Prerequisites: `K.1-C.5`.


This lesson isolates **Vector representation of lines/planes** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a High skill: it is standard GRE material. You should be able to do it without warming up. Geometry is additional topics plus analytic geometry inside calculus/algebra items.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Vector representation of lines/planes**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Analytic/vector geometry*

Point-line and point-plane distances, parametric lines, Cartesian planes, dot for angles, cross for area/normal, projections, quadratic forms as conics.

### This skill, specifically

Point-line and point-plane distances, parametric lines, Cartesian planes, dot for angles, cross for area/normal, projections, quadratic forms as conics.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

A line through $\mathbf{p}$ with direction $\mathbf{d}\neq\mathbf{0}$ is $\mathbf{r}(t)=\mathbf{p}+t\mathbf{d}$, or $(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{p})\times\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{0}$. A plane through $\mathbf{p}$ with normal $\mathbf{n}$ is $\mathbf{n}\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{p})=0$. A plane through $\mathbf{p}$ spanned by independent $\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}$ is $\mathbf{r}(s,t)=\mathbf{p}+s\mathbf{u}+t\mathbf{v}$, with $\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}$.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Parametric equations of the line through $(1,2,3)$ and $(3,2,1)$.

**Solution.** $\mathbf{r}(t)=(1,2,3)+t(2,0,-2)$, or $x=1+2t$, $y=2$, $z=3-2t$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** Plane through $(1,0,0),(0,1,0),(0,0,1)$: cartesian equation

**Solution.** $x+y+z=1$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Writing a unique parametric form as if $t$ were canonical (reparametrization $\mathbf{d}\mapsto 2\mathbf{d}$ is the same line).
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Normal to the plane $\mathbf{r}=(1,0,0)+s(0,1,0)+t(0,0,1)$.

**Solution.** $(1,0,0)$ (the $yz$-plane through $x=1$).

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Symmetric equations of $\mathbf{r}=(0,0,0)+t(2,3,6)$.

**Solution.** $x/2=y/3=z/6$.

## 9. Lock it in

You own this skill when you can do Drill 1 and Drill 2 tomorrow, mixed among unrelated problems, in under five minutes each, with the traps named in Section 7 avoided on purpose.
