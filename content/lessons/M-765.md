# Lesson M-765 — Counting by complement

Part R. R. Combinatorics · **Counting principles** · High

Prerequisites: `Q.4`.


This lesson isolates **Counting by complement** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a High skill: it is standard GRE material. You should be able to do it without warming up. Combinatorics is additional topics and also probability setup.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Counting by complement**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Counting principles*

Add disjoint options, multiply independent successive choices, divide by overcount. Factorials, $P(n,k)$, $C(n,k)$, multinomial, repetition, circular permutations, complement counting.

### This skill, specifically

Add disjoint options, multiply independent successive choices, divide by overcount.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

Counting by complement: $|S\setminus A|=|S|-|A|$. Use when `at least one' or `not all' is messier than the forbidden set. Combine with inclusion-exclusion when the bad set is a union.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** How many length-$6$ binary strings are not the zero string?

**Solution.** $2^6-1=63$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** From $\binom{9}{4}$ $4$-subsets of $\{1,\ldots,9\}$, how many contain at least one even? (evens: $2,4,6,8$).

**Solution.** Complement: subsets of the $5$ odds: $\binom{9}{4}-\binom{5}{4}=126-5=121$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Subtracting only one bad type when several bad types overlap, or subtracting from the wrong universe.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Positive integers $\le 100$ not divisible by $7$.

**Solution.** $100-\lfloor 100/7\rfloor=100-14=86$.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** $5$-letter words from $\{A,B,C\}$ that use at least two distinct letters.

**Solution.** $3^5-3=243-3=240$ (subtract the three constant words).

You own this skill when you can do Drill 1 and Drill 2 tomorrow, mixed among unrelated problems, in under five minutes each, with the traps named in Section 7 avoided on purpose.
