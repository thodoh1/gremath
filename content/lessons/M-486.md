# Lesson M-486 — Cayley-Hamilton theorem

Part K. K. Linear Algebra · **Eigenvalues & eigenvectors** · Critical

Prerequisites: `K.6`.


This lesson isolates **Cayley-Hamilton theorem** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a Critical skill: it is used constantly, and a hole here leaks points all over the paper. Linear algebra is a large share of the algebra portion. Rank, kernels, eigenvalues, and determinants are exam staples.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Cayley-Hamilton theorem**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Eigenvalues & eigenvectors*

$Av=\lambda v$ with $v\neq 0$. Characteristic polynomial, algebraic vs geometric multiplicity, diagonalization iff enough independent eigenvectors. Trace is sum of eigenvalues; det is product. Cayley–Hamilton.

### This skill, specifically

$Av=\lambda v$ with $v\neq 0$.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

Cayley–Hamilton: if $p_A(\lambda)=\det(A-\lambda I)=(-1)^n\lambda^n+\cdots$, then $p_A(A)=0$ (the zero matrix). Every power $A^k$ for $k\ge n$ is a linear combination of $I,A,\ldots,A^{n-1}$. Useful for computing $A^{-1}$ from the adjugate identity when $p_A(0)\neq 0$.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Verify CH for $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$.

**Solution.** $p_A(\lambda)=(1-\lambda)^2=\lambda^2-2\lambda+1$. $A^2=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$, $A^2-2A+I=0$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** For $2\times 2$ $A$, $A^2-(\mathrm{tr}\,A)A+(\det A)I$ equals

**Solution.** $0$ (CH with $p(\lambda)=\lambda^2-(\mathrm{tr})\lambda+\det$, matching $\det(\lambda I-A)$).

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Substituting $A$ into $p_A$ entrywise as if $p_A(A)=(p_A(a_{ij}))$, or claiming CH says $p_A(\lambda)=0$ for each eigenvalue only (true but weaker).
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** If $A^2=0$ for $n\times n$ $A$, then every eigenvalue is

**Solution.** $0$, so $p_A(\lambda)=(-\lambda)^n$.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** CH implies $A^{-1}$ is a polynomial in $A$ when

**Solution.** $\det A\neq 0$ (constant term of $p_A$ nonzero).
