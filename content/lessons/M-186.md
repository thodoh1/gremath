# Lesson M-186 — Roots of unity

Part D. D. Complex Numbers · **Polar/exponential complex form** · High

Prerequisites: `D.1-C.1`.


This lesson isolates **Roots of unity** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a High skill: it is standard GRE material. You should be able to do it without warming up. Complex numbers appear in algebra, polynomials, eigenvalues, and as a bridge to Part W.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Roots of unity**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Polar/exponential complex form*

$z=re^{i\theta}=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)$. Multiplication adds arguments and multiplies moduli. De Moivre computes powers. The $n$th roots of a nonzero complex number are $n$ equally spaced points on a circle. Roots of unity are the unit-circle special case.

### This skill, specifically

$z=re^{i\theta}=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)$.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

The $n$th roots of unity are the solutions of $z^n=1$: $\omega_k=e^{2\pi i k/n}$, $k=0,\ldots,n-1$. They form a cyclic group under multiplication. A primitive $n$th root generates the group. $1+\omega+\cdots+\omega^{n-1}=0$ if $\omega\neq 1$ is an $n$th root of unity.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** The primitive cube roots of unity.

**Solution.** $\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}=-\frac12+i\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ and $\omega^2=e^{-2\pi i/3}$, with $1+\omega+\omega^2=0$ and $\omega^3=1$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** Sum of all $5$th roots of unity

**Solution.** $0$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Calling $-1$ a primitive $4$th root (order of $-1$ is $2$, not $4$).
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Is $i$ a primitive $4$th root of unity?

**Solution.** Yes: $i, i^2=-1, i^3=-i, i^4=1$.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** How many primitive $6$th roots of unity?

**Solution.** $\phi(6)=2$: $e^{2\pi i/6}=e^{i\pi/3}$ and $e^{5i\pi/3}$.
