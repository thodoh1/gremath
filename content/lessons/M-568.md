# Lesson M-568 — Isomorphism

Part M. M. Group Theory · **Homomorphisms & quotients** · High

Prerequisites: `M.3`.


This lesson isolates **Isomorphism** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a High skill: it is standard GRE material. You should be able to do it without warming up. Group theory is the main abstract-algebra GRE diet: orders, Lagrange, homomorphisms.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Isomorphism**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Homomorphisms & quotients*

A homomorphism preserves the operation. Kernel is a normal subgroup. First isomorphism theorem: $G/\ker\phi\cong\mathrm{im}\,\phi$. Quotients exist precisely because of normality.

### This skill, specifically

A homomorphism preserves the operation.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

An isomorphism is a bijective homomorphism. Write $G\cong K$. Isomorphism preserves order, being abelian/cyclic, numbers of elements of each order, subgroup lattices up to iso, and solvability of equations $x^n=e$. $\mathbb{Z}/2\times\mathbb{Z}/2\not\cong\mathbb{Z}/4$ (max order $2$ vs $4$). $(\mathbb{R},+)\cong(\mathbb{R}_{>0},\cdot)$ via $\exp$. Cayley: $G\cong$ a subgroup of $S_G$.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Are $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ isomorphic? Are $U(5)$ and $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$?

**Solution.** No: $\mathbb{Z}/4$ has an element of order $4$; Klein does not. $U(5)=\{1,2,3,4\}$ is cyclic of order $4$ generated by $2$, so yes $\cong\mathbb{Z}/4$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** $S_3\cong D_3$ (symmetries of the triangle)?

**Solution.** Yes.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Matching only the orders of the groups and declaring them isomorphic.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Is $(\mathbb{Q},+)\cong(\mathbb{Q}_{>0},\cdot)$?

**Solution.** No. Additive $\mathbb{Q}$ is divisible: $2x=a$ always has a solution. In $(\mathbb{Q}_{>0},\cdot)$ the equation $x^2=2$ has none. (Equivalently $\mathbb{Q}_{>0}^\times$ is free abelian on the primes.)

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** $\mathbb{Z}\cong n\mathbb{Z}$ via $k\mapsto nk$.

**Solution.** Yes, for $n\neq 0$.

## 9. Lock it in

You own this skill when you can do Drill 1 and Drill 2 tomorrow, mixed among unrelated problems, in under five minutes each, with the traps named in Section 7 avoided on purpose.
