# Lesson M-424 — Orthogonality

Part K. K. Linear Algebra · **Vectors & geometry** · Critical

Prerequisites: `A.1`.


This lesson isolates **Orthogonality** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a Critical skill: it is used constantly, and a hole here leaks points all over the paper. Linear algebra is a large share of the algebra portion. Rank, kernels, eigenvalues, and determinants are exam staples.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Orthogonality**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Vectors & geometry*

Vectors add, scale, dot (angles, projections), cross in $\mathbb{R}^3$ (area, orthogonality), triple product (volume). Geometry and algebra are the same object.

### This skill, specifically

Vectors add, scale, dot (angles, projections), cross in $\mathbb{R}^3$ (area, orthogonality), triple product (volume).

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

Nonzero vectors are orthogonal iff $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=0$ (include $\mathbf{0}$ by convention: $\mathbf{0}$ is orthogonal to every vector). A set is orthogonal if every pair is. Orthogonal nonzero vectors are linearly independent.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Are $(1,2,-1)$ and $(2,-1,0)$ orthogonal?

**Solution.** Dot product $2-2+0=0$, yes.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** A vector orthogonal to both $\mathbf{i}$ and $\mathbf{j}$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$ is parallel to

**Solution.** $\mathbf{k}$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Concluding orthogonality from $|\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}|^2=|\mathbf{u}|^2+|\mathbf{v}|^2$ without recognizing this is equivalent (parallelogram / Pythagoras), or using the cross product being zero (that is parallel, the opposite).
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Is $(1,1,1)$ orthogonal to $(1,-2,1)$?

**Solution.** Yes, dot $0$.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Can three nonzero pairwise orthogonal vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$ exist?

**Solution.** No: at most $2$.

## 9. Lock it in

You own this skill when you can do Drill 1 and Drill 2 tomorrow, mixed among unrelated problems, in under five minutes each, with the traps named in Section 7 avoided on purpose.
