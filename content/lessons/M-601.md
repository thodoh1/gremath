# Lesson M-601 — Rational root test

Part N. N. Rings, Modules & Fields · **Polynomial rings & factorization** · Medium

Prerequisites: `N.1-N.2`.


This lesson isolates **Rational root test** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a Medium skill: it appears less often, but when it appears it is often a discriminator. Rings and fields appear as recognition and finite examples, especially $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ and $F[x]$.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Rational root test**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Polynomial rings & factorization*

$F[x]$ is a Euclidean domain, hence a PID and UFD. Irreducibility tests, rational root theorem, division. CRT for rings: $R/(I\cap J)\cong R/I\times R/J$ when $I+J=R$.

### This skill, specifically

$F[x]$ is a Euclidean domain, hence a PID and UFD.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

Rational root test: if $f=a_n x^n+\cdots+a_0\in\mathbb{Z}[x]$ and $p/q$ in lowest terms is a rational root, then $p\mid a_0$ and $q\mid a_n$. This produces a finite list of candidates to test. It never proves irreducibility of degree $\ge 4$ by itself; it can prove there is no rational linear factor.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Possible rational roots of $2x^3+x^2-7x-6$, and test them.

**Solution.** Candidates $p\mid 6$, $q\mid 2$: $\pm 1,2,3,6,\frac12,\frac32$. $f(-1)=-2+1+7-6=0$, so $x+1$ divides. Synthetic division: $2x^3+x^2-7x-6=(x+1)(2x^2-x-6)=(x+1)(2x+3)(x-2)$. Rational roots $-1,2,-3/2$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** Possible rational roots of $x^3+x+1$

**Solution.** $\pm 1$. Neither works: $1+1+1=3$, $-1-1+1=-1$. No rational root.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Testing $p/q$ with $p$ dividing the leading coefficient instead of the constant term.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Possible rational roots of $x^3-x^2-8x+12$.

**Solution.** $\pm 1,2,3,4,6,12$. $x=2$: $8-4-16+12=0$, so $2$ is a root.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Does the test list $2/3$ for $3x^2-2$?

**Solution.** Yes: $p\mid -2$, $q\mid 3$, including $2/3$. And $3(4/9)-2=-2/3\neq 0$; $f(\sqrt{2/3})$ is the real root, irrational.

## 9. Lock it in

You own this skill when you can do Drill 1 and Drill 2 tomorrow, mixed among unrelated problems, in under five minutes each, with the traps named in Section 7 avoided on purpose.
