## 3. Precise statement

Cauchy criterion for series: $\sum a_n$ converges iff the tails are uniformly small: for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $N$ so that $n\ge N$ and $p\ge 1$ imply $|a_{n+1}+\cdots+a_{n+p}|<\varepsilon$. Equivalent to $(s_n)$ being Cauchy, hence to completeness of $\mathbb{R}$.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Use Cauchy's criterion to show $\sum 1/n$ diverges.

**Solution.** The block from $n+1$ to $2n$ is $\ge 1/2$, so tails are not Cauchy.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** For a geometric series with $|r|<1$, the tail $\sum_{k=n+1}^\infty r^k$ equals

**Solution.** $r^{n+1}/(1-r)\to 0$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Checking only $p=1$ (i.e. $a_n\to 0$) and calling it the Cauchy criterion.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** If Cauchy criterion holds, does $a_n\to 0$?

**Solution.** Yes ($p=1$).

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Why is completeness used here?

**Solution.** $(s_n)$ Cauchy in $\mathbb{R}$ converges.
