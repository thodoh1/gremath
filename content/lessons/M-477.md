# Lesson M-477 — Eigenvector definition

Part K. K. Linear Algebra · **Eigenvalues & eigenvectors** · Critical

Prerequisites: `K.6`.


This lesson isolates **Eigenvector definition** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a Critical skill: it is used constantly, and a hole here leaks points all over the paper. Linear algebra is a large share of the algebra portion. Rank, kernels, eigenvalues, and determinants are exam staples.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Eigenvector definition**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Eigenvalues & eigenvectors*

$Av=\lambda v$ with $v\neq 0$. Characteristic polynomial, algebraic vs geometric multiplicity, diagonalization iff enough independent eigenvectors. Trace is sum of eigenvalues; det is product. Cayley–Hamilton.

### This skill, specifically

$Av=\lambda v$ with $v\neq 0$.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

An eigenvector for $\lambda$ is a nonzero $\mathbf{v}$ with $A\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}$, i.e. a nontrivial element of $\ker(A-\lambda I)$. Scalar multiples of $\mathbf{v}$ (nonzero) are eigenvectors for the same $\lambda$. Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues of any matrix are linearly independent.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** An eigenvector of $\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$ for $\lambda=3$.

**Solution.** $A-3I=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}$, so $y=0$, $\mathbf{v}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** If $A\mathbf{v}=4\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}$, then $A(7\mathbf{v})$ equals

**Solution.** $4(7\mathbf{v})$: still an eigenvector.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Reporting $\mathbf{0}$ as the eigenvector found by row reduction, or mixing left eigenvectors $\mathbf{w}^T A=\lambda\mathbf{w}^T$ without saying so.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Eigenvector of $\mathrm{diag}(4,-1)$ for $\lambda=-1$.

**Solution.** $\mathbf{e}_2$ (or multiples).

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Can $\mathbf{v}$ be an eigenvector for two different eigenvalues?

**Solution.** No (unless $\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}$, which is forbidden).

## 9. Lock it in

You own this skill when you can do Drill 1 and Drill 2 tomorrow, mixed among unrelated problems, in under five minutes each, with the traps named in Section 7 avoided on purpose.
