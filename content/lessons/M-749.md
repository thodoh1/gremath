# Lesson M-749 — Uncountability

Part Q. Q. Logic, Sets & Proof · **Functions & cardinality** · High

Prerequisites: `Q.4`.


This lesson isolates **Uncountability** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a High skill: it is standard GRE material. You should be able to do it without warming up. Logic and proof are how other items are worded. Counting and analysis both depend on quantifiers.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Uncountability**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Functions & cardinality*

Injection, surjection, bijection, composition, inverses. Countable vs uncountable, Cantor diagonal, $|\mathbb{N}|=|\mathbb{Z}|=|\mathbb{Q}|<|\mathbb{R}|$, axiom of choice as awareness.

### This skill, specifically

Injection, surjection, bijection, composition, inverses.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

A set is uncountable if it is not countable, i.e. there is no injection into $\mathbb{N}$. $\mathbb{R}$, $[0,1]$, $\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})$, $\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}$, and $C[0,1]$ are uncountable. Uncountable sets can have different cardinalities; $|\mathbb{R}|=2^{\aleph_0}=\mathfrak{c}$. Continuum hypothesis ($2^{\aleph_0}=\aleph_1$) is independent of ZFC.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Deduce that $\mathbb{R}$ is uncountable from the countability of $\mathbb{Q}$ and the existence of irrationals? Why is that insufficient, and what actually works?

**Solution.** Insufficient: $\mathbb{Q}\cup\{\sqrt{2}\}$ is still countable. Need Cantor's diagonal argument (or $|\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})|=2^{\aleph_0}$ and $\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}$).

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** $[0,1]$ is uncountable: true?

**Solution.** True, in bijection with $\mathbb{R}$ (e.g. via $\tan$ on $(0,1)$ plus junk at endpoints).

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Thinking every uncountable set is in bijection with $\mathbb{R}$.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Is $\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}$ uncountable?

**Solution.** Yes: otherwise $\mathbb{R}$ would be a countable union of countable sets.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** $|\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})|$ vs $|\mathbb{N}|$.

**Solution.** Strictly larger (Cantor's theorem).

## 9. Lock it in

You own this skill when you can do Drill 1 and Drill 2 tomorrow, mixed among unrelated problems, in under five minutes each, with the traps named in Section 7 avoided on purpose.
