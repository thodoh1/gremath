# Lesson M-482 — Diagonalization

Part K. K. Linear Algebra · **Eigenvalues & eigenvectors** · Critical

Prerequisites: `K.6`.


This lesson isolates **Diagonalization** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a Critical skill: it is used constantly, and a hole here leaks points all over the paper. Linear algebra is a large share of the algebra portion. Rank, kernels, eigenvalues, and determinants are exam staples.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Diagonalization**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Eigenvalues & eigenvectors*

$Av=\lambda v$ with $v\neq 0$. Characteristic polynomial, algebraic vs geometric multiplicity, diagonalization iff enough independent eigenvectors. Trace is sum of eigenvalues; det is product. Cayley–Hamilton.

### This skill, specifically

$Av=\lambda v$ with $v\neq 0$.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

$A$ is diagonalizable if $A=PDP^{-1}$ for diagonal $D$ and invertible $P$. Equivalently $A$ has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors (the columns of $P$), equivalently $\gamma(\lambda)=\alpha(\lambda)$ for every eigenvalue. Over $\mathbb{C}$, having $n$ distinct eigenvalues suffices but is not necessary.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Diagonalize $\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}$.

**Solution.** $\lambda=4,2$. $\mathbf{v}_4=(1,1)$, $\mathbf{v}_2=(1,-1)$. $P=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}$, $D=\mathrm{diag}(4,2)$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** Is $\begin{pmatrix}5&1\\0&5\end{pmatrix}$ diagonalizable?

**Solution.** No: $\gamma(5)=1<2$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Writing $A=PDP$ without the inverse, or putting eigenvectors in $D$.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** A $2\times 2$ matrix with eigenvalues $4$ and $-1$ is

**Solution.** diagonalizable (distinct eigenvalues).

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** $P^{-1}AP=D$ means the basis of columns of $P$

**Solution.** consists of eigenvectors.

## 9. Lock it in

You own this skill when you can do Drill 1 and Drill 2 tomorrow, mixed among unrelated problems, in under five minutes each, with the traps named in Section 7 avoided on purpose.
