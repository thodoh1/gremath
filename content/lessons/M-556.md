# Lesson M-556 — Composition of permutations

Part M. M. Group Theory · **Permutation groups** · High

Prerequisites: `M.1-Q.3`.


This lesson isolates **Composition of permutations** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a High skill: it is standard GRE material. You should be able to do it without warming up. Group theory is the main abstract-algebra GRE diet: orders, Lagrange, homomorphisms.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Composition of permutations**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Permutation groups*

$S_n$ is the group of bijections on $n$ letters. Cycle decomposition, disjoint cycles commute, transpositions generate, sign homomorphism, $A_n=\ker(\mathrm{sign})$.

### This skill, specifically

$S_n$ is the group of bijections on $n$ letters.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

Composition of permutations is function composition. Convention used here: $(\sigma\tau)(x)=\sigma(\tau(x))$ — apply $\tau$ first, then $\sigma$ (right to left). To multiply cycles, track each number through the right factor then the left. Inverse reverses cycles. Always state the convention; the other convention (left to right) reverses all products.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Compute $(1\,2)(1\,3)$ with right-to-left composition.

**Solution.** Apply $(1\,3)$ first, then $(1\,2)$: $1\mapsto 3\mapsto 3$, $3\mapsto 1\mapsto 2$, $2\mapsto 2\mapsto 1$, so $(1\,3\,2)$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** $(1\,2\,3)(1\,2)$ (right first)

**Solution.** Apply $(1\,2)$ then $(1\,2\,3)$: $1\mapsto 2\mapsto 3$, $2\mapsto 1\mapsto 2$, $3\mapsto 3\mapsto 1$, so $(1\,3)$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Mixing left-to-right and right-to-left in the same computation.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** $(1\,2\,3)^2$.

**Solution.** $(1\,3\,2)$.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Inverse of $(1\,4\,2)(3\,5)$.

**Solution.** $(2\,4\,1)(3\,5)=(1\,2\,4)(3\,5)$.

## 9. Lock it in

You own this skill when you can do Drill 1 and Drill 2 tomorrow, mixed among unrelated problems, in under five minutes each, with the traps named in Section 7 avoided on purpose.
