# Lesson M-968 — Roundoff error

Part X. X. Numerical Analysis · **Numerical error** · Medium

Prerequisites: `A.1`.


This lesson isolates **Roundoff error** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a Medium skill: it appears less often, but when it appears it is often a discriminator. Numerical methods are additional topics: Newton, error, quadrature recognition.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Roundoff error**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 3. Precise statement

Roundoff error is the error from representing numbers with finitely many digits and from rounding after arithmetic. In floating point, $x\oplus y=(x+y)(1+\delta)$ with $|\delta|\le\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}$ typically. Subtractive cancellation: $x-y$ for $x\approx y$ loses significant digits (absolute error stays, relative error explodes). Summation order can change the rounded result.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** In four-digit rounding, compute $(1.000+1.000\times 10^{-4})-1.000$ vs $1.000\times 10^{-4}$.

**Solution.** The first may yield $0$ after $(1.000+0.0001)\to 1.000$; the second is $0.0001$. Cancellation/rounding destroyed the increment.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** Evaluating $\sqrt{1+x}-1$ for tiny $x$ is unstable; a stable rewrite is

**Solution.** $x/(\sqrt{1+x}+1)$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Blaming truncation of a Taylor series for a digit-cancellation bug, or assuming floating-point addition is associative.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Significant digits lost when subtracting $1.234$ from $1.235$ in four-digit arithmetic.

**Solution.** About three digits (result $0.001$ has one significant digit as written).

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Why is $(a+b)+c$ not always equal to $a+(b+c)$ in floating point?

**Solution.** Different rounding of partial sums.
