## 3. Precise statement

Counting $\leftrightarrow$ probability: if all outcomes in a finite $S$ are equally likely, $P(A)=|A|/|S|$. Binomial: $P(k\text{ successes})=\binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$. ‘Number of ways’ is a numerator; independence is a product of counts over a product space. Complement: $1-P(A)$ often beats inclusion of many cells. Distinguish permutations vs combinations before writing $\binom{n}{k}$.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Fair coin, $4$ tosses. $P(\text{exactly two heads})$.

**Solution.** $\binom{4}{2}/16=6/16=3/8$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** $5$ cards from $52$: $P(\text{all hearts})=$

**Solution.** $\binom{13}{5}/\binom{52}{5}$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Writing $\binom{n}{k}$ when order matters (passwords, sequences) or $n^k$ when order does not.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Number of $5$-letter strings from $\{A,B,C\}$ with exactly two $A$s.

**Solution.** $\binom{5}{2}2^3=80$ (choose positions for $A$, the rest $B$ or $C$).

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Two fair dice: $P(\text{sum}=7)$ via counting

**Solution.** $6/36=1/6$.
