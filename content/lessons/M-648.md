# Lesson M-648 — Alternating series

Part O. O. Real Analysis · **Series & convergence** · High

This lesson isolates **Alternating series** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a High skill: it is standard GRE material. You should be able to do it without warming up. Real analysis items are often ‘which statement is true?’ definition checks inside additional topics.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Alternating series**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 3. Precise statement

Leibniz alternating series test: if $b_n\ge 0$ is decreasing and $b_n\to 0$, then $\sum_{n=1}^\infty (-1)^{n+1} b_n$ converges. The remainder after $N$ terms has the sign of the next term and absolute value $\le b_{N+1}$.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Does $\sum_{n=2}^\infty (-1)^n\frac{\ln n}{n}$ converge?

**Solution.** Yes: $(\ln n)/n$ decreases to $0$ eventually (e.g. for $n\ge 3$), so Leibniz applies. Not absolutely ($p=1$ with log).

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** Error after the first four terms of $1-1/2+1/3-1/4+\cdots$ is at most

**Solution.** $1/5$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Using Leibniz when $b_n$ is not eventually monotone ($b_n=1/n$ if $n$ odd, $1/n^2$ if even).
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Does $\sum (-1)^n (1+1/n)$ converge?

**Solution.** No: terms $\not\to 0$.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Must $b_n$ be decreasing from $n=1$?

**Solution.** No: eventually decreasing is enough.
