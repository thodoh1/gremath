## 3. Precise statement

The adjacency matrix $A$ of a simple labeled graph is the $n\times n$ symmetric $0$-$1$ matrix with $A_{ij}=1$ iff $ij\in E$, and $0$ diagonal. Then $(A^k)_{ij}$ equals the number of walks of length $k$ from $i$ to $j$. $\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}=2|E|$.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Adjacency matrix of $P_3$ with vertices labeled $1{-}2{-}3$ in path order.

**Solution.** $\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\1&0&1\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix}$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** $(A^2)_{11}$ for that $P_3$.

**Solution.** $1$: the walk $1$-$2$-$1$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Putting $2$ on the diagonal for degree, or using an asymmetric $A$ for an undirected graph without saying so.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

**Drill 1.** Trace of $A$ for a simple graph.

**Solution.** $0$.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** $A^3_{ij}$ counts

**Solution.** walks of length $3$ from $i$ to $j$.
