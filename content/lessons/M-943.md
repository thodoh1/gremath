# Lesson M-943 — Complex limits

Part W. W. Complex Analysis · **Complex functions** · Medium

Prerequisites: `D.1-O.4`.


This lesson isolates **Complex limits** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a Medium skill: it appears less often, but when it appears it is often a discriminator. Complex analysis is additional topics: Cauchy–Riemann, residues at a basic level.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Complex limits**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Complex functions*

### This skill, specifically

$f:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}$, limits and continuity as in $\mathbb{R}^2$, complex differentiability is much stronger, holomorphic $=$ analytic in this course’s setting, Cauchy–Riemann, harmonic real and imaginary parts.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

$\lim_{z\to z_0}f(z)=L$ means: for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $\delta>0$ such that $0<|z-z_0|<\delta$ implies $|f(z)-L|<\varepsilon$. The limit must agree along every path in $\mathbb{C}$. If two paths give different limits, the limit does not exist. This is stricter than separate real limits of $u$ and $v$ along axes only.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Show $\lim_{z\to 0}\overline{z}/z$ does not exist.

**Solution.** Along the real axis the quotient is $1$; along the imaginary axis it is $-1$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** $\lim_{z\to i}(z^2+1)$ equals

**Solution.** $0$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Checking only $z=x$ and $z=iy$, then declaring a limit that actually fails along $y=x$ (classic: $\overline{z}/z$ is the usual counterexample; $xy/(x^2+y^2)$ style examples for $\mathbb{R}^2$).
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Does $\lim_{z\to 0}z\sin(1/|z|)$ exist?

**Solution.** Yes, $0$, by $|z\sin(1/|z|)|\le|z|$.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** If $\lim f=L$ and $\lim g=M$, then $\lim(f+g)=$

**Solution.** $L+M$.
