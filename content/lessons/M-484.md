# Lesson M-484 — Trace as sum of eigenvalues

Part K. K. Linear Algebra · **Eigenvalues & eigenvectors** · Critical

Prerequisites: `K.6`.


This lesson isolates **Trace as sum of eigenvalues** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a Critical skill: it is used constantly, and a hole here leaks points all over the paper. Linear algebra is a large share of the algebra portion. Rank, kernels, eigenvalues, and determinants are exam staples.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Trace as sum of eigenvalues**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Eigenvalues & eigenvectors*

$Av=\lambda v$ with $v\neq 0$. Characteristic polynomial, algebraic vs geometric multiplicity, diagonalization iff enough independent eigenvectors. Trace is sum of eigenvalues; det is product. Cayley–Hamilton.

### This skill, specifically

$Av=\lambda v$ with $v\neq 0$.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

The trace $\mathrm{tr}\,A=\sum_i a_{ii}$ equals the sum of eigenvalues counted with algebraic multiplicity (over $\mathbb{C}$). Also $\mathrm{tr}(AB)=\mathrm{tr}(BA)$, hence $\mathrm{tr}(P^{-1}AP)=\mathrm{tr}\,A$. Trace is linear. For $2\times 2$, $p_A(\lambda)=\lambda^2-(\mathrm{tr}\,A)\lambda+\det A$ up to the $A-\lambda I$ sign convention.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Eigenvalues of $\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{pmatrix}$ sum to what? Check by trace.

**Solution.** $\mathrm{tr}=5$. (Product $\det=-2$.)

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Summing only the distinct eigenvalues when a repeated eigenvalue should be counted twice.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.
