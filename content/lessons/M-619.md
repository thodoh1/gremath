# Lesson M-619 — Transcendental element

Part N. N. Rings, Modules & Fields · **Fields & extensions** · Medium

Prerequisites: `N.3-L.2`.


This lesson isolates **Transcendental element** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a Medium skill: it appears less often, but when it appears it is often a discriminator. Rings and fields appear as recognition and finite examples, especially $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ and $F[x]$.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Transcendental element**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Fields & extensions*

A field has every nonzero element invertible. Extensions $K/F$ have degree $[K:F]=\dim_F K$. Algebraic vs transcendental, minimal polynomials, splitting fields, $\mathbb{F}_p$ and $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$.

### This skill, specifically

A field has every nonzero element invertible.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

An element $\alpha$ is transcendental over $F$ if it is not algebraic: no nonzero polynomial in $F[x]$ vanishes at $\alpha$. Then $F[\alpha]\cong F[x]$ and $F(\alpha)\cong F(x)$, and $[F(\alpha):F]=\infty$. Over $\mathbb{Q}$, $e$ and $\pi$ are transcendental (Hermite, Lindemann). Transcendence of $\pi$ implies a circle cannot be squared with ruler and compass. $x\in F(x)$ is transcendental over $F$.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Why is $[F(x):F]=\infty$? Show $1,x,x^2,\ldots$ are linearly independent over $F$.

**Solution.** A finite linear combination $\sum_{k=0}^n a_k x^k=0$ in $F(x)$ is a polynomial identity, so all $a_k=0$. Hence infinitely many independent powers, infinite degree.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** Is $\pi^2$ transcendental over $\mathbb{Q}$?

**Solution.** Yes: if algebraic, $\pi$ would be algebraic (a square root of an algebraic is algebraic).

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Concluding $\alpha$ transcendental because it is irrational (algebraic irrationals exist: $\sqrt{2}$).
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** $e+\pi$ is known to be transcendental?

**Solution.** Open. At least one of $e+\pi$, $e\pi$ is transcendental.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** $F(\pi)/F$ for $F=\mathbb{R}$. Is $\pi$ transcendental over $\mathbb{R}$?

**Solution.** No: $\pi\in\mathbb{R}$, min poly $x-\pi$, algebraic of degree $1$.
