# Lesson M-878 — Percentiles

Part U. U. Mathematical Statistics · **Descriptive statistics** · Medium

Prerequisites: `T.2`.


This lesson isolates **Percentiles** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a Medium skill: it appears less often, but when it appears it is often a discriminator. Statistics is lighter than probability but bias, MLE, and tests do appear.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Percentiles**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 3. Precise statement

The $k$th percentile is the $p=k/100$ quantile. Saying ‘the $90$th percentile is $12$’ means about $90\%$ of the mass lies at or below $12$. Percentile rank of a value $x$ is $100\,F(x)$ (with a sample convention for ties).

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** For $N(10,4^2)$, the $50$th percentile is

**Solution.** $10$ (the median of a symmetric unimodal law).

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** If $F(7)=0.8$, then $7$ is the

**Solution.** $80$th percentile.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Mixing ‘percentile’ with percent change, or thinking the $90$th percentile of $N(0,1)$ is $0.90$.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** $25$th percentile of $\mathrm{Unif}[10,20]$.

**Solution.** $12.5$.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** In an ordered sample of size $10$, a common $10$th percentile is near

**Solution.** $x_{(1)}$ or a weighted blend of $x_{(1)}$ and $x_{(2)}$, depending on the plotting-position rule.
