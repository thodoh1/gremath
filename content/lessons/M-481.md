# Lesson M-481 — Geometric multiplicity

Part K. K. Linear Algebra · **Eigenvalues & eigenvectors** · Critical

Prerequisites: `K.6`.


This lesson isolates **Geometric multiplicity** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a Critical skill: it is used constantly, and a hole here leaks points all over the paper. Linear algebra is a large share of the algebra portion. Rank, kernels, eigenvalues, and determinants are exam staples.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Geometric multiplicity**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Eigenvalues & eigenvectors*

$Av=\lambda v$ with $v\neq 0$. Characteristic polynomial, algebraic vs geometric multiplicity, diagonalization iff enough independent eigenvectors. Trace is sum of eigenvalues; det is product. Cayley–Hamilton.

### This skill, specifically

$Av=\lambda v$ with $v\neq 0$.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

The geometric multiplicity $\gamma(\lambda)=\dim E_\lambda=\dim\ker(A-\lambda I)=n-\mathrm{rank}(A-\lambda I)$. Always $1\le\gamma(\lambda)\le\alpha(\lambda)$ when $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue. $A$ fails to be diagonalizable iff some $\gamma(\lambda)<\alpha(\lambda)$. A Jordan block of size $k$ has $\gamma=1$ and $\alpha=k$.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Geometric multiplicity of $\lambda=4$ for $\begin{pmatrix}4&1\\0&4\end{pmatrix}$.

**Solution.** $A-4I=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$, rank $1$, $\gamma(4)=2-1=1<\alpha(4)=2$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** If $A=I_n$, $\gamma(1)$ is

**Solution.** $n$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Computing $n-\mathrm{rank}(A)$ instead of $n-\mathrm{rank}(A-\lambda I)$.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Can $\gamma(\lambda)>\alpha(\lambda)$?

**Solution.** Never.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Diagonalizable iff for every eigenvalue

**Solution.** $\gamma(\lambda)=\alpha(\lambda)$.
