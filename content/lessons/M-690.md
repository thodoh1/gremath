# Lesson M-690 — Compactness

Part P. P. Topology · **Separation & compactness** · Medium

Prerequisites: `P.1-P.2`.


This lesson isolates **Compactness** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a Medium skill: it appears less often, but when it appears it is often a discriminator. Topology appears as compactness, connectedness, open/closed, Heine–Borel.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Compactness**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Separation & compactness*

Hausdorff: points can be separated by opens. Compact: every open cover has a finite subcover. Continuous images of compact sets are compact. In $\mathbb{R}^n$, compact $\Leftrightarrow$ closed and bounded (Heine–Borel).

### This skill, specifically

Hausdorff: points can be separated by opens.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

$X$ is compact if every open cover of $X$ has a finite subcover. Finite spaces are compact. $[0,1]$ is compact; $(0,1)$ and $\mathbb{R}$ are not. Closed subsets of compact spaces are compact; compact subsets of Hausdorff spaces are closed. Continuous images of compact sets are compact.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Give an open cover of $(0,1)$ with no finite subcover.

**Solution.** $\{(1/n,1):n\ge 2\}$: the union is $(0,1)$, and any finite subcollection has a largest $1/N$, missing $(0,1/N]$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** A closed subset of a compact space is

**Solution.** compact.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Calling a set compact because it is closed, or because it is bounded, without Heine--Borel hypotheses.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Is a finite union of compact sets compact?

**Solution.** Yes.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Infinite discrete space: compact?

**Solution.** No: cover by singletons has no finite subcover.

## 9. Lock it in

You own this skill when you can do Drill 1 and Drill 2 tomorrow, mixed among unrelated problems, in under five minutes each, with the traps named in Section 7 avoided on purpose.
