# Lesson M-003 — Scientific notation and order of magnitude

Part A. A. Foundations & Algebraic Fluency · **Arithmetic & number manipulation** · Critical

Prerequisites: `none — this is ground floor`.


No calculator means you live in powers of ten.

This is a Critical skill: it is used constantly, and a hole here leaks points all over the paper. Algebraic fluency is not a listed ETS bucket of its own, but it is the cost of every calculus and algebra item. Expect it to be invisible when you have it and fatal when you do not.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Scientific notation and order of magnitude**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Arithmetic & number manipulation*

Arithmetic is the field operations on $\mathbb{Z}\subset\mathbb{Q}\subset\mathbb{R}$. Integers add and multiply and subtract; rationals add division (except by zero); reals add limits of rationals, which you will meet as completeness in Part O. On the GRE there is no calculator, so every later topic — limits, matrices, congruences, probabilities — is only as strong as this fluency. Train until a fraction, a sign, a floor, and a bound are boring.

### This skill, specifically

Huge and tiny numbers are the same skill as exponents. The mantissa carries the significant digits; the exponent carries the scale.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

Scientific notation writes $x=m\times 10^e$ with $1\le |m|<10$ and $e\in\mathbb{Z}$. Order of magnitude is the nearest power of ten, or sometimes $\lfloor\log_{10}|x|\rfloor$.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Move the point until the mantissa is in $[1,10)$, counting the moves as $e$. To multiply, multiply mantissas and add exponents, then renormalize.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Write $0.00047$ in scientific notation.

**Solution.** $4.7\times 10^{-4}$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** $(3.0\times 10^8)(2.0\times 10^{-3})$ equals

**Solution.** $6.0\times 10^{5}$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Counting decimal places off-by-one.
- Forgetting to renormalize after multiplying mantissas: $30	imes 10^4$ is not scientific form.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Which is larger, $2.1\times 10^{-3}$ or $8\times 10^{-4}$?

**Solution.** $2.1\times 10^{-3}=21\times 10^{-4}>8\times 10^{-4}$.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Approximate $7!\times 10^{-4}$ to one significant digit in scientific notation.

**Solution.** $7!=5040$, so about $5\times 10^{-1}$.

## 9. Lock it in

You own this skill when you can do Drill 1 and Drill 2 tomorrow, mixed among unrelated problems, in under five minutes each, with the traps named in Section 7 avoided on purpose.
