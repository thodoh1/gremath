# Lesson M-187 — Geometric arrangement of roots

Part D. D. Complex Numbers · **Polar/exponential complex form** · High

Prerequisites: `D.1-C.1`.


This lesson isolates **Geometric arrangement of roots** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a High skill: it is standard GRE material. You should be able to do it without warming up. Complex numbers appear in algebra, polynomials, eigenvalues, and as a bridge to Part W.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Geometric arrangement of roots**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Polar/exponential complex form*

$z=re^{i\theta}=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)$. Multiplication adds arguments and multiplies moduli. De Moivre computes powers. The $n$th roots of a nonzero complex number are $n$ equally spaced points on a circle. Roots of unity are the unit-circle special case.

### This skill, specifically

$z=re^{i\theta}=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)$.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

The $n$th roots of a nonzero $w$ are the vertices of a regular $n$-gon on the circle $|z|=|w|^{1/n}$, one of them being a chosen polar $n$th root. Rotating by $2\pi/n$ cycles them.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Where do the cube roots of $i$ lie?

**Solution.** On the circle of radius $1$, at angles $(\pi/2+2\pi k)/3$ for $k=0,1,2$, i.e. $\pi/6$, $5\pi/6$, $3\pi/2$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** The fourth roots of $16$ form

**Solution.** a square with vertices $2,-2,2i,-2i$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Drawing the polygon at radius $|w|$ instead of $|w|^{1/n}$.
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Angle spacing of $5$th roots of $32$.

**Solution.** $2\pi/5$, radius $2$.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Do the $n$th roots of $-1$ include $1$?

**Solution.** Only if $n$ is even? $1^n=1\neq -1$. Never. They include $-1$ when $n$ is odd.

## 9. Lock it in

You own this skill when you can do Drill 1 and Drill 2 tomorrow, mixed among unrelated problems, in under five minutes each, with the traps named in Section 7 avoided on purpose.
