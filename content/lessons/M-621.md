# Lesson M-621 — Irreducibility connection

Part N. N. Rings, Modules & Fields · **Fields & extensions** · Medium

Prerequisites: `N.3-L.2`.


This lesson isolates **Irreducibility connection** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a Medium skill: it appears less often, but when it appears it is often a discriminator. Rings and fields appear as recognition and finite examples, especially $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ and $F[x]$.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Irreducibility connection**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Fields & extensions*

A field has every nonzero element invertible. Extensions $K/F$ have degree $[K:F]=\dim_F K$. Algebraic vs transcendental, minimal polynomials, splitting fields, $\mathbb{F}_p$ and $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$.

### This skill, specifically

A field has every nonzero element invertible.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

Connection: $\alpha$ algebraic over $F$ of degree $n$ iff $m_\alpha$ is irreducible of degree $n$ iff $\{1,\alpha,\ldots,\alpha^{n-1}\}$ is an $F$-basis of $F(\alpha)$. Adjoining a root of an irreducible $f$ of degree $n$ produces an extension of degree $n$. Reducible polynomials still have roots in some extension, but adjoining one root of a reducible polynomial gives degree equal to an irreducible factor's degree.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Degree of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$ and of $\mathbb{Q}(\omega\sqrt[3]{2})$ where $\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}$. Both roots of $x^3-2$.

**Solution.** Both have degree $3$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ because $x^3-2$ is irreducible (Eisenstein $2$). The splitting field $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2},\omega)$ has degree $6$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** If $f$ irreducible of degree $n$ and $f(\alpha)=0$, then $[F(\alpha):F]=$

**Solution.** $n$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Adjoining a root of a reducible polynomial and claiming the degree is $\deg f$ (it is the degree of the irr factor that actually vanishes).
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** $x^4-1$ at $\alpha=-1$: $[\mathbb{Q}(-1):\mathbb{Q}]=$

**Solution.** $1$, because the irr factor is $x+1$ of degree $1$.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Can an irreducible cubic have a root in a quadratic extension?

**Solution.** No: degree $3$ cannot divide $2$.
