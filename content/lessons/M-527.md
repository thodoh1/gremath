# Lesson M-527 — Integer-factorization arguments

Part L. L. Number Theory · **Diophantine equations** · High

Prerequisites: `L.1-L.2`.


This lesson isolates **Integer-factorization arguments** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a High skill: it is standard GRE material. You should be able to do it without warming up. Number theory is additional-topics / algebra: congruences, Euler, CRT.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Integer-factorization arguments**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Diophantine equations*

$ax+by=c$ is solvable iff $\gcd(a,b)\mid c$. General integer solutions follow Bézout. Modular obstructions kill many equations instantly. Pythagorean triples and Pell are extra pattern libraries.

### This skill, specifically

$ax+by=c$ is solvable iff $\gcd(a,b)\mid c$.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

Integer-factorization arguments: rewrite a Diophantine equation so both sides factor, then use that $\mathbb{Z}$ is a UFD. Typical: $x^2-y^2=(x-y)(x+y)$, $n^2-1=(n-1)(n+1)$, $xy=n$ with $x\le y$ enumerates divisor pairs. Equal-factor constraints plus parity/gcd finish the list.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Find all positive $x>y>0$ of the same parity with $x^2-y^2=15$.

**Solution.** $15=(x-y)(x+y)$. Factor pairs of the same parity: $(1,15)$, $(3,5)$ (both odd). $x-y=1$, $x+y=15\Rightarrow x=8,y=7$. And $x-y=3$, $x+y=5\Rightarrow x=4,y=1$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** Positive pairs $(x,y)$ with $xy=12$, $x\le y$

**Solution.** $(1,12),(2,6),(3,4)$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Forgetting that $x-y$ and $x+y$ must both be even or both odd (same parity as $2x,2y$).
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** Integer solutions of $x(x+1)=6$.

**Solution.** $x=2$ and $x=-3$ (because $(-3)(-2)=6$).

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Positive $m$ with $m(m+2)=24$.

**Solution.** $m=4$: factors of $24$ differing by $2$ are $4$ and $6$.

## 9. Lock it in

You own this skill when you can do Drill 1 and Drill 2 tomorrow, mixed among unrelated problems, in under five minutes each, with the traps named in Section 7 avoided on purpose.
