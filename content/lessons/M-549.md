# Lesson M-549 — Permutations as bijections

Part M. M. Group Theory · **Permutation groups** · High

Prerequisites: `M.1-Q.3`.


This lesson isolates **Permutations as bijections** and trains it until it is automatic.

This is a High skill: it is standard GRE material. You should be able to do it without warming up. Group theory is the main abstract-algebra GRE diet: orders, Lagrange, homomorphisms.

## 1. Why this skill exists

If you cannot do **Permutations as bijections**, every later item that secretly uses it becomes a two-minute panic. The GRE will not name the skill. It will drop you into a computation or a “which statement is true?” and wait.

## 2. From zero

### Landscape of *Permutation groups*

$S_n$ is the group of bijections on $n$ letters. Cycle decomposition, disjoint cycles commute, transpositions generate, sign homomorphism, $A_n=\ker(\mathrm{sign})$.

### This skill, specifically

$S_n$ is the group of bijections on $n$ letters.

You are not learning the whole topic again. You are learning the exact move named in the title. Neighboring lessons exist; use them if a word here is still foreign, then come back.

## 3. Precise statement

A permutation of a set $X$ is a bijection $X\to X$. The set $S_X$ of permutations is a group under composition (the symmetric group). For $X=\{1,\ldots,n\}$, write $S_n$. $|S_n|=n!$. The identity permutation fixes every point. Two-row notation lists images of $1,\ldots,n$.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 4. How a working mathematician uses it

Write the hypotheses, apply the move, check the conclusion against the question asked.

On the exam, this should feel like a reflex: see the pattern, apply the move, check hypotheses, box the quantity asked for.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** How many permutations of $\{1,2,3,4\}$ send $1$ to $2$? Write one in two-row form.

**Solution.** $3!=6$ (the rest may be any permutation of the remaining three). Example: $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2&3&4\\2&1&4&3\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** A permutation is injective. Why is it then automatically surjective on a finite set?

**Solution.** Finite set, injective $\Rightarrow$ surjective.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Counting functions $\{1,\ldots,n\}\to\{1,\ldots,n\}$ as $n!$ (that is $n^n$; permutations are the bijections).
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** $|S_5|$.

**Solution.** $120$.

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Is the map $1\mapsto 2,2\mapsto 3,3\mapsto 2$ a permutation of $\{1,2,3\}$?

**Solution.** No: not injective/surjective ($1$ has no preimage, $2$ hit twice).

## 9. Lock it in

You own this skill when you can do Drill 1 and Drill 2 tomorrow, mixed among unrelated problems, in under five minutes each, with the traps named in Section 7 avoided on purpose.
