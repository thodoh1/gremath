## 3. Precise statement

The least common multiple $\mathrm{lcm}(a,b)$ is the smallest positive common multiple. For $a,b\neq 0$, $\gcd(a,b)\cdot\mathrm{lcm}(a,b)=|ab|$. $\mathrm{lcm}$ is a multiple of each; any common multiple is a multiple of the lcm.

Copy that statement onto paper and replace each symbol with a tiny example. A definition you cannot instantiate is a definition you do not have.

## 5. Worked example

**Problem.** Find $\mathrm{lcm}(12,18)$ two ways.

**Solution.** Multiples of $12$: $12,24,36,\ldots$; of $18$: $18,36,\ldots$. So $36$. Check: $\gcd(12,18)=6$ and $6\cdot 36=216=12\cdot 18$.

Now cover the solution and redo it. If you cannot, you are still in Section 2.

## 6. GRE-style problem

**Problem.** $\mathrm{lcm}(8,12)$ equals

**Solution.** $24$.

Notice what was *not* done: no extra theory, no calculator, no essay. The governing idea, then the computation.

## 7. Traps that cost points

- Using $\mathrm{lcm}(a,b)=ab$ even when $\gcd>1$ (that overcounts).
- Skip checking a candidate in the original equation.

## 8. Drills

Work these closed-book. Then check.

**Drill 1.** $\mathrm{lcm}(7,3)$.

**Solution.** $21$ (coprime, so product).

Cover that solution, redo the drill on paper, then continue.

**Drill 2.** Does $\mathrm{lcm}(6,10,15)=30$?

**Solution.** Yes: $30$ is a common multiple and no smaller positive one exists.
